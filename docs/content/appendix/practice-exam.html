A random variable X has expected value \mathrm{E}[X] = 2\theta + 6 and variance \mathrm{Var}(X) = 2\theta^2.
A random variable Y has expected value \mathrm{E}[Y] = 3\theta - 12 and variance \mathrm{Var}(Y) = 6\theta^2 + 1.
Assume X and Y are independent.

  1. Compute the bias, variance, and MSE of the following estimators of \theta:

    1. \hat{\theta}_1 = 5X - 3Y - 65
    2. \hat{\theta}_2 = (X+Y)/5
    3. \hat{\theta}_3 = Y/6 + X/4 + 1/2
    [9 marks]

  2. Let \hat{\theta} = aX + bY + c be an estimator of \theta. Derive a system of linear equations involving a, b, c that ensures \hat{\theta} is unbiased for any value of \theta. [4 marks]

  3. Suppose \hat{\theta} = aX + bY + c is an unbiased estimator of \theta. Find the values of a, b, c that minimise the mean squared error of \hat{\theta} when the true value of \theta = 1. [7 marks]

a. Bias, variance, and MSE.

\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta, \qquad \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2.

Using independence of X and Y:

\mathrm{Var}(aX+bY+c) = a^2\mathrm{Var}(X) + b^2\mathrm{Var}(Y).

\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c} \text{Estimator } \hat{\theta} & \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] & \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}] & \text{Bias} & \text{MSE} \\ \hline 5X - 3Y - 65 & \theta+1 & 104\theta^2+9 & 1 & 104\theta^2+10 \\ (X+Y)/5 & \theta-\tfrac{6}{5} & \tfrac{8\theta^2+1}{25} & -\tfrac{6}{5} & \tfrac{8\theta^2+37}{25} \\ Y/6 + X/4 + 1/2 & \theta & \tfrac{7}{24}\theta^2 + \tfrac{1}{36} & 0 & \tfrac{7}{24}\theta^2 + \tfrac{1}{36} \\ \end{array}

b. Unbiasedness condition.

\mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] = a\,\mathrm{E}[X] + b\,\mathrm{E}[Y] + c = a(2\theta+6) + b(3\theta-12) + c.

For unbiasedness: \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] = \theta for all \theta.

So (2a+3b-1)\theta + (6a - 12b + c) = 0.

This gives the system: 2a+3b=1, \qquad 6a - 12b + c = 0.

c. Minimising MSE when \theta=1.

For an unbiased estimator, MSE = variance:

\mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}] = a^2 \mathrm{Var}(X) + b^2 \mathrm{Var}(Y) = 2a^2 \theta^2 + b^2(6\theta^2+1).

At \theta=1: \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}] = 2a^2 + 7b^2.

From part (b): a = \tfrac{1-3b}{2}, c = 12b - 6a.

Substitute a=(1-3b)/2: \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}] = 2\Big(\tfrac{1-3b}{2}\Big)^2 + 7b^2 = \tfrac{(1-3b)^2}{2} + 7b^2 = \tfrac{23}{2}b^2 - 3b + \tfrac{1}{2}.

Minimise by setting derivative =0:
23b - 3 = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad b = \tfrac{3}{23}.

Then a = \tfrac{1-3b}{2} = \tfrac{7}{23}, \qquad c = 12b - 6a = -\tfrac{6}{23}.

Final recommended estimator: \hat{\theta} = \frac{7X + 3Y - 6}{23}.

Suppose that X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n, for n \geq 2 and \lambda>0, are independent \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda) random variables.

Two possible estimators of \lambda are: \hat{\lambda}_1 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \qquad \hat{\lambda}_2 = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i.

  1. Show that \hat{\lambda}_1 is unbiased but \hat{\lambda}_2 is not. [3 marks]

  2. Show that \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_1) = \frac{\lambda}{n}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_2) = \frac{\lambda}{n^3}. [3 marks]

  3. For each estimator, determine whether it can be shown to be consistent using its mean and variance. [4 marks]

a. Unbiasedness.

\mathrm{E}[\hat{\lambda}_1] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[X_i] = \frac{1}{n} \cdot n\lambda = \lambda.

So \hat{\lambda}_1 is unbiased.

\mathrm{E}[\hat{\lambda}_2] = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[X_i] = \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot n\lambda = \frac{\lambda}{n}.

So \hat{\lambda}_2 is biased for n\ge 2.

b. Variances.

Since X_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda), \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \lambda.

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_1) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot n\lambda = \frac{\lambda}{n}.

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_2) = \frac{1}{n^4}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{1}{n^4} \cdot n\lambda = \frac{\lambda}{n^3}.

c. Consistency.

  • \hat{\lambda}_1: unbiased and \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_1)\to 0 as n\to\infty, so \hat{\lambda}_1 is consistent.
  • \hat{\lambda}_2: its mean tends to 0 and its variance tends to 0, so the same sufficient condition shows that \hat{\lambda}_2 converges in probability to 0. Since \lambda>0, it is not consistent for \lambda.

Suppose we have a single Binomial observation: X \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, \theta).

  1. Derive the expected Fisher information \mathcal I(\theta). [4 marks]
  2. Derive the observed information j(\theta;x). [3 marks]
  3. Evaluate both at the MLE when 0<x<n. [3 marks]

We compute \mathcal I(\theta)=-\mathrm E_\theta[\ell''(\theta;X)] and j(\theta;x)=-\ell''(\theta;x). The former averages over possible data; the latter is the curvature of the realised log-likelihood.

Step 1: Compute log-likelihood

Since we have a single observation, the likelihood is just the Binomial PMF: L(\theta ; x) = \binom{n}{x} \theta^x(1-\theta)^{n-x}. Therefore, the log-likelihood takes the form \ell(\theta; x) = \log L(\theta ; x) = \log\left(\binom{n}{x}\right) + x \log \theta + (n-x)\log(1-\theta).

Step 2: Compute Derivatives

We compute the first and second derivatives of the log-likelihood with respect to \theta: \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \ell(\theta; x) &= \frac{x}{\theta} - \frac{n-x}{1-\theta} \\ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} \ell(\theta; x) &= -\frac{x}{\theta^2} - \frac{n-x}{(1-\theta)^2}. \end{align*}

Step 3: Compute Fisher Information

We have \mathcal{I}(\theta) = -\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2} \ell(\theta; X)\right] = -\mathrm{E}\left[-\frac{X}{\theta^2} - \frac{n-X}{(1-\theta)^2}\right], where the random component of the expectation is X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n, \theta). Therefore, manipulating the expectation: \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}(\theta) &= \mathrm{E}\left[\frac{X}{\theta^2} + \frac{n-X}{(1-\theta)^2}\right] \\ &= \frac{1}{\theta^2}\mathrm{E}[X] + \frac{1}{(1-\theta)^2} \mathrm{E}[n - X] \\ &= \frac{n\theta}{\theta^2} + \frac{n - n\theta}{(1-\theta)^2}, \end{align*} using the fact that \mathrm{E}[X] = n\theta. Continuing: \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}(\theta) &= \frac{n}{\theta} + \frac{n}{1-\theta} \\ &= \frac{n(1-\theta) + n\theta}{\theta(1-\theta)} \\ &= \frac{n}{\theta(1-\theta)}. \end{align*}

The observed information is therefore j(\theta;x)=\frac{x}{\theta^2}+\frac{n-x}{(1-\theta)^2}.

Step 4: Compute MLE

For 0<x<n, the interior MLE solves: \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \ell(\theta; x) &= 0 \\ \frac{x}{\theta} - \frac{n-x}{1-\theta} &= 0 \\ x(1-\theta) &= \theta(n-x) \\ x &= n\theta \\ \Rightarrow \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}} &= \frac{x}{n}. \end{align*}

Step 5: Evaluate both information measures at the MLE

The plug-in expected information is \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}}) &= \frac{n}{\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}}(1-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}})} \\ &= \frac{n}{x/n(1-x/n)} \\ &= \frac{n^3}{x(n - x)}. \end{align*} In this model the observed information happens to agree at the MLE: j(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}};x) =\frac{x}{(x/n)^2}+\frac{n-x}{(1-x/n)^2} =\frac{n^3}{x(n-x)}. This equality is model-specific; the two definitions should not be conflated.

  1. Approximate CI. Note that, in order to compute the approximate CI we require both the MLE of p and the Fisher information.
    • Step 1: Compute Log Likelihood For i.i.d. geometric data, the likelihood is \begin{align*} L(p; \underline{x}) &= \prod_{i=1}^n p(1-p)^{x_i - 1} \\ &= p^n(1-p)^{n(\bar{x} - 1)}. \end{align*} The log-likelihood is thus \ell(p; \underline{x}) = n \log p + n(\bar{x} - 1) \log(1-p).
    • Step 2: Compute Derivatives of the Log-Likelihood The derivatives of the log-likelihood are \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial p} \ell(p; \underline{x}) &= \frac{n}{p} - \frac{n(\bar{x} - 1)}{1 - p}, \\ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial p^2} \ell(p; \underline{x}) &= -\frac{n}{p^2} - \frac{n(\bar{x} - 1)}{(1 - p)^2}. \end{align*}
    • Step 3: Compute MLE We compute the MLE by solving \begin{align*} \frac{n}{p} - \frac{n(\bar{x} - 1)}{1 - p} &= 0 \\ n(1-p) - n(\bar{x} - 1)p &= 0 \\ n\bar{x} p &= n \\ \Rightarrow \hat{p}_{\mathrm{MLE}} &= \frac{1}{\bar{x}}. \end{align*}
    • Step 4: Compute Fisher Information The Fisher information for the whole sample is: \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}_n(p) &= -\mathrm{E}\left[-\frac{n}{p^2} - \frac{n(\bar{X} - 1)}{(1 - p)^2}\right] \\ &= \frac{n}{p^2} - \frac{n}{(1-p)^2} + \frac{n}{(1-p)^2}\mathrm{E}[\bar{X}] \\ &= \frac{n}{p^2} - \frac{n}{(1-p)^2} + \frac{n}{p(1-p)^2}, \end{align*} since \mathrm{E}[X_i] = 1/p and so \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[X_i] = 1/p. Simplifying further, we obtain \mathcal{I}_n(p) = n\frac{(1-p)^2 - p^2 + p}{p^2(1-p)^2} = \frac{n}{p^2(1-p)}. Substituting p = \hat{p}_{\mathrm{MLE}} = 1/\bar{x} into the expected Fisher information gives \mathcal{I}_n(\hat{p}_{\mathrm{MLE}}) = \frac{n \bar{x}^2}{(1-1/\bar{x})} = \frac{n\bar{x}^3}{\bar{x} - 1}. For this model, the realised observed information evaluated at the MLE has the same value, but the two definitions are conceptually distinct.
    • Step 5: Compute Approximate CI For an interior true parameter 0<p<1 and large n, the MLE has approximate sampling distribution \hat{p}_{\mathrm{MLE}} \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\bigl(p, \mathcal{I}_n(p)^{-1}\bigr). Evaluating the information at the observed MLE gives the estimated standard error. When \bar{x}>1, the resulting approximate 100(1-\alpha)\% confidence interval is \frac{1}{\bar{x}} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\bar{x} - 1}{n \bar{x}^3}}.
  2. Compute the Approximate CI using data. Using the derived formula from the first part \frac{1}{\bar{x}} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\bar{x} - 1}{n \bar{x}^3}}. we plug in the given values \bar{x} = 9.84 and n=100. The relevant quantile is z_{0.975} = 1.96. Therefore, we obtain 0.1016 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0096 \approx 0.1016 \pm 0.0189. Therefore, an approximate 95\% confidence interval for p is \boxed{ (0.083, 0.121), \quad \text{(to three decimal places)}. }

A call centre manager claims that the average number of customer complaint calls received per hour is 5. To assess this claim, the number of complaint calls is recorded over a random sample of n = 60 one-hour periods. Across these 60 hours, a total of X = 360 complaint calls are received.

Assuming a Poisson model X_i \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda), \qquad i=1,\dots,n, where \lambda is the true mean number of complaint calls per hour, use the Central Limit Theorem to perform a 1% significance level two-sided hypothesis test of the manager’s claim.

You may use z_{0.995}=2.576.

Let X_1,\dots,X_n be the hourly counts. The sample mean is \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i = \frac{360}{60} = 6.

For a Poisson random variable, \mathrm{E}[X_i]=\lambda and \mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\lambda. For large n, the CLT gives \bar{X} \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\left(\lambda, \frac{\lambda}{n}\right).

Step 1: State the hypotheses

The claim “\lambda = 5 per hour” corresponds to H_0: \lambda = 5, \qquad H_1: \lambda \neq 5. This is a two-sided test.

Step 2: Compute the test statistic

Under the null, the standard error is \mathrm{SE}_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda_0}{n}} = \sqrt{\frac{5}{60}} \approx 0.289.

The observed test statistic is T_{\mathrm{obs}} = \frac{\bar{X} - \lambda_0}{\mathrm{SE}_0} = \frac{6 - 5}{0.289} \approx 3.46.

Step 3: Determine the rejection region / p-value

At the 1% significance level for a two-sided test, the critical values are \pm z_{0.995} = \pm 2.576.

Since |3.46| > 2.576, the test statistic falls in the rejection region.

Equivalently, the p-value is 2\,\mathrm{Pr}(Z \ge 3.46) \approx 2(0.00027) \approx 0.00054.

Step 4: Conclusion

There is sufficient evidence at the 1% significance level to reject the manager’s claim. The data provide evidence that the true mean number of complaint calls per hour is different from 5.

For n\ge 2, suppose that X_1, \ldots, X_n are independent \mathcal{N}(\mu, \theta) random variables, where \mu is a known constant and \theta is the unknown variance. Let \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i.

  1. Let \hat{\theta} = \frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 be an estimator of \theta.

    1. Determine the distribution of \bar{X} and hence show that
      \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}] = \mu and \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}^2] = \theta/n + \mu^2.

    2. Show that \mathrm{E}[X_i^2] = \theta + \mu^2 for any i = 1, \ldots, n.

    3. Using the fact that \mathrm{E}[X_i\bar{X}] = \theta/n + \mu^2 for any i, or by another method,
      derive the value of c that ensures \hat{\theta} is unbiased.

    [7 marks]

  2. Show that for any unbiased estimator \hat{\theta} of \theta, it holds that \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}] \geq \frac{2\theta^2}{n}. [6 marks]

  3. Let \hat{\theta}' = \frac{1}{n+2}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2 be another estimator of \theta.

    Determine whether \hat{\theta}' has a lower mean-squared error (MSE) than any unbiased estimator of \theta.

    Hint: If X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \theta), then \mathrm{Var}[(X-\mu)^2] = 2\theta^2. [7 marks]

(1a)
Since \bar{X} is the sample mean of i.i.d. normals, it follows that
\bar{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \theta/n).
Hence: \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}] = \mu, \qquad \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}^2] = \mathrm{Var}[\bar{X}] + \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}]^2 = \frac{\theta}{n} + \mu^2.

(1b)
For any i, \mathrm{E}[X_i^2] = \mathrm{Var}[X_i] + \mathrm{E}[X_i]^2 = \theta + \mu^2.

(1c)
We have: \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] = \frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[(X_i - \bar{X})^2] = \frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=1}^n \Big(\mathrm{E}[X_i^2] - 2\mathrm{E}[X_i\bar{X}] + \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}^2]\Big).

Substituting known expectations: \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] = \frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=1}^n \big(\theta+\mu^2 - 2(\theta/n+\mu^2) + \theta/n + \mu^2\big) = \frac{n-1}{c}\theta.

Thus, \hat{\theta} is unbiased if and only if c = n-1.

(2)

The likelihood is: L(\theta) = (2\pi\theta)^{-n/2} \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\mu)^2\right].

Hence the log-likelihood: \ell(\theta) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\log(\theta) - \frac{1}{2\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\mu)^2.

Differentiating twice: \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \theta^2} = \frac{n}{2\theta^2} - \frac{1}{\theta^3}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2.

The Fisher information is: \mathcal{I}(\theta) = -\mathrm{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \theta^2}\right] = \frac{n}{2\theta^2}.

By the Cramér–Rao inequality: \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}] \ge \frac{1}{\mathcal{I}(\theta)} = \frac{2\theta^2}{n}.

(3)

Compute the bias: \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}'] = \frac{1}{n+2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[(X_i - \mu)^2] = \frac{n\theta}{n+2}, \qquad \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}') = -\frac{2\theta}{n+2}.

Variance: \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}'] = \frac{1}{(n+2)^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}[(X_i - \mu)^2] = \frac{2n\theta^2}{(n+2)^2}.

Hence: \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}') = \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}'] + \mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}')^2 = \frac{2n\theta^2}{(n+2)^2} + \frac{4\theta^2}{(n+2)^2} = \frac{2\theta^2}{n+2}.

Since \frac{2\theta^2}{n+2} < \frac{2\theta^2}{n} for all n > 0,
\hat{\theta}' has a smaller mean-squared error than any unbiased estimator of \theta.

Let X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n be independent and identically distributed random variables from the Uniform distribution: X_i \sim U(\theta, 2\theta), \qquad \theta > 0.

Take the density to be 1/\theta on the closed interval [\theta,2\theta] and zero outside it. Obtain the maximum likelihood estimator \hat{\theta}.

Step 1: Write down the PDF

The probability density function is
f(x \mid \theta) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{\theta}, & \theta \le x \le 2\theta, \\[6pt] 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}

Step 2: Construct the likelihood

For a random sample x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n,
L(\theta; \underline{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \theta) = \begin{cases} \theta^{-n}, & \theta \le \min(x_i), \; 2\theta \ge \max(x_i), \\[4pt] 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}

This condition can be rewritten as
\frac{1}{2}\max(x_i) \le \theta \le \min(x_i).

Step 3: Identify the MLE

Within this interval, L(\theta) = \theta^{-n}, which is a decreasing function of \theta.
Hence, the likelihood is maximized by taking the smallest allowable value of \theta: \hat{\theta} = \frac{1}{2}\max(X_i).

Step 4: Conclusion

The maximum likelihood estimator is
\boxed{\hat{\theta} = \tfrac{1}{2}\max(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n).}

Let Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_n denote these counts, assumed IID with Y_i\sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda), \qquad i = 1,\dots,n, where \lambda is the true mean number of visits per period.

  1. Explain what it means to “estimate \lambda’’ in the frequentist framework, and how uncertainty about \lambda is represented in the Bayesian framework. [2 marks]

  2. The ecologists want to test whether the feeder changes bird activity, using H_0 : \lambda = \lambda_0 \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad H_1 : \lambda \neq \lambda_0. Because \lambda is unknown and the data are Poisson, they decide to use a large-sample Central Limit Theorem (CLT) approximation to construct a test.

    1. Define the size (significance level) of a hypothesis test. [2 marks]

    2. Define the rejection region of a size-\alpha test. [2 marks]

    3. Let \bar{Y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i. Using the CLT, show that under H_0, \sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{Y} - \lambda_0}{\sqrt{\lambda_0}} \ \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim}\ \mathcal{N}(0,1) \qquad\text{for large } n. Hence show that a size-\alpha two-sided test rejects H_0 when \bar{Y} < \lambda_0 - k_\alpha \quad \text{or} \quad \bar{Y} > \lambda_0 + k_\alpha, where k_\alpha > 0 is chosen appropriately. [5 marks]

    4. For the rejection region obtained in part (b)(iii), derive the approximate power function \beta(\lambda) = \Pr(\text{Reject } H_0 \mid \lambda), and express your answer in terms of the standard Normal CDF \Phi(\cdot). [5 marks]

  3. In a particular study, the ecologists take n = 25 observation periods. They wish to test H_0 : \lambda = 5 \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad H_1 : \lambda \neq 5 using the CLT-based test from part (b) at the 1\% significance level.

    Over the 25 periods, the total number of bird visits recorded is \sum_{i=1}^{25} Y_i = 175.

    Using the approximate test derived in part (b):

    1. compute the observed value of the test statistic;

    2. state the critical value(s) for a two-sided test at the 1\% level;

    3. determine whether H_0 should be rejected, and briefly interpret your conclusion in the context of the study.

    [4 marks]

You may use the standard Normal quantile z_{0.995} = 2.576.

(a) In frequentist inference, \lambda is fixed and unknown. We estimate it with a statistic such as \bar{Y} and use the statistic’s sampling distribution to quantify uncertainty over repeated samples. In Bayesian inference, the posterior distribution of \lambda, formed by updating a prior using the data, represents uncertainty about its value.

(b)(i) With this simple null hypothesis, the size is the probability of rejecting H_0 when \lambda=\lambda_0, the Type I error probability.

(b)(ii) The rejection region is the set of sample outcomes for which the test rejects H_0. For a test of size \alpha, that set has probability \alpha under H_0.

(b)(iii) The Poisson model gives \mathrm{E}[\bar{Y}]=\lambda and \mathrm{Var}(\bar{Y})=\lambda/n. Setting \lambda=\lambda_0 in the CLT gives Z_0=\frac{\bar{Y}-\lambda_0}{\sqrt{\lambda_0/n}} \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1). A two-sided test of approximate size \alpha rejects for |Z_0|>z_{1-\alpha/2}. Equivalently, it rejects when \bar{Y}<\lambda_0-k_\alpha or \bar{Y}>\lambda_0+k_\alpha, where k_\alpha=z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\lambda_0/n}.

(b)(iv) At the true rate \lambda>0, use the approximation \bar{Y}\overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\lambda,\lambda/n). Standardising the two rejection tails with this mean and variance gives \begin{aligned} \beta(\lambda)&\approx \Phi\!\left(\frac{\lambda_0-k_\alpha-\lambda}{\sqrt{\lambda/n}}\right)\\ &\quad+1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{\lambda_0+k_\alpha-\lambda}{\sqrt{\lambda/n}}\right). \end{aligned}

(c)(i) The sample mean is 175/25=7, so the observed statistic is (7-5)/\sqrt{5/25}=10/\sqrt{5}\approx 4.47.

(c)(ii) The 1\% two-sided critical values are -2.576 and 2.576.

(c)(iii) Since 4.47>2.576, reject H_0. There is evidence that the mean number of visits differs from five per period. The observed mean suggests a higher visit rate after installing the feeder.

A young hobbit proposes the estimator \hat{\mu}_1 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i, while another suggests the alternative \hat{\mu}_2 = \frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i.

  1. Show that \hat{\mu}_1 is an unbiased estimator of \mu, and determine the bias of \hat{\mu}_2. [3 marks]

  2. Compute \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_1) and \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_2). [3 marks]

  3. For each estimator, compute the mean squared error (MSE). If the true mean were \mu=0, which estimator has the smaller MSE for finite n? [2 marks]

  4. State, with a brief justification, whether \hat{\mu}_1 and \hat{\mu}_2 are consistent estimators of \mu. [2 marks]

  1. By linearity, the means are \mathrm{E}[\hat\mu_1]=\mu and \mathrm{E}[\hat\mu_2]=n\mu/(n+1). Therefore \hat\mu_1 is unbiased and the bias of \hat\mu_2 is -\mu/(n+1).

  2. Independence gives \mathrm{Var}(\sum_{i=1}^n X_i)=n\sigma^2. Dividing by the squared denominators gives \mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu_2)=\frac{n\sigma^2}{(n+1)^2}.

  3. Adding variance and squared bias gives \mathrm{MSE}(\hat\mu_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \mathrm{MSE}(\hat\mu_2)=\frac{n\sigma^2+\mu^2}{(n+1)^2}. When \mu=0, the second MSE is the first multiplied by (n/(n+1))^2, so \hat\mu_2 has the smaller MSE if \sigma^2>0. Both MSEs are zero in the degenerate case \sigma^2=0.

  4. Both MSEs tend to zero as n\to\infty. For any \varepsilon>0 the probability that either estimator differs from \mu by more than \varepsilon is bounded by its MSE divided by \varepsilon^2, so both estimators are consistent.

In the garden of Lothlórien, Galadriel invites Frodo to gaze into her Mirror. The Mirror displays a continuous sequence of visions. The distribution of X is modelled as X \sim \mathrm{NegBin}(r,p), with probability mass function \Pr(X = x \mid p) = \binom{x + r - 1}{r - 1} p^r (1-p)^x, \qquad x = 0,1,2,\ldots, where 0 < p < 1. It can be shown that \mathrm{E}[X] = \frac{r(1-p)}{p}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \frac{r(1-p)}{p^2}.

  3. Galadriel possesses ancient wisdom regarding the clarity of the Mirror. She places a \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta) prior on p, with density \pi(p) \propto p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}, \qquad 0 < p < 1.

    2. Hence write down the posterior mean and variance of p.

      Using your expression for \mathcal{I}_n(p) from part (b), derive the asymptotic posterior distribution for p.

      Comment on the connection between the Bayesian posterior and the frequentist large-sample distribution of the MLE. [5 marks]

(a) Let S=\sum_i x_i. By independence, the likelihood is L(p;\underline{x}) =p^{nr}(1-p)^S\prod_{i=1}^n\binom{x_i+r-1}{r-1}. Its log has derivative nr/p-S/(1-p). Equating this to zero gives the estimate nr/(nr+S). Because S>0, the solution is interior, and strict concavity gives a maximum. Writing the result in terms of the random sample yields \hat{p}=\frac{r}{r+\bar{X}}.

(b) For a single observation the terms in the log-likelihood that depend on p are r\log p+X\log(1-p). Differentiating twice gives -r/p^2-X/(1-p)^2. Using the stated mean of X, the information is \mathcal{I}_1(p)=\frac{r}{p^2}+\frac{\mathrm{E}[X]}{(1-p)^2} =\frac{r}{p^2}+\frac{r}{p(1-p)} =\frac{r}{p^2(1-p)}. The log-likelihoods add over independent observations, so \mathcal{I}_n(p)=nr/[p^2(1-p)].

(c)(i) Multiplying likelihood and prior gives \pi(p\mid\underline{x})\propto p^{\alpha+nr-1}(1-p)^{\beta+S-1}. The posterior is therefore \mathrm{Beta}(a,b) with a=\alpha+nr and b=\beta+S.

(c)(ii) The posterior mean and variance are \mathrm{E}[p\mid\underline{x}]=\frac{a}{a+b}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(p\mid\underline{x})=\frac{ab}{(a+b)^2(a+b+1)}. The prior parameters are fixed, so for large n the posterior mean a/(a+b) is close to \hat{p}=nr/(nr+S) and a+b+1 is close to nr+S=nr/\hat{p}. Writing the posterior variance as [a/(a+b)][b/(a+b)]/(a+b+1) gives a leading term of \hat{p}^{\,2}(1-\hat{p})/(nr), the inverse Fisher information at \hat{p}. The resulting Normal approximation is p\mid\underline{x}\overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{p},\,\frac{\hat{p}^{\,2}(1-\hat{p})}{nr}\right). In the frequentist sampling distribution, \hat{p} is random and centred approximately at the true p_0, with approximate variance p_0^2(1-p_0)/(nr). Both approximations use inverse Fisher information. The posterior treats the data as fixed and uses \hat p for its centre and variance calculation.
