Important 1: Events: at least one, exactly one, or neither

For a randomly selected student, let A be the event that they attend a revision session and B the event that they complete a practice paper. Suppose \mathrm{Pr}(A)=0.60,\qquad \mathrm{Pr}(B)=0.45,\qquad \mathrm{Pr}(A\cap B)=0.25.

  1. Find the probability that the student does at least one of these activities.
  2. Find the probability that they do neither activity.
  3. Find the probability that they do exactly one activity. Explain why this differs from part (a).
Solution

(a) Inclusion–exclusion gives \mathrm{Pr}(A\cup B)=0.60+0.45-0.25=0.80. The union includes students who do both activities.

(b) Neither activity is the complement of the union, so its probability is 1-0.80=0.20.

(c) The two disjoint possibilities are attending only and completing only: \mathrm{Pr}(A\cap B^c)+\mathrm{Pr}(A^c\cap B) =(0.60-0.25)+(0.45-0.25)=0.55. Exactly one excludes the intersection. As a check, the four probabilities for attending only, completing only, both, and neither sum to 0.35+0.20+0.25+0.20=1.

Important 2: Conditional probabilities from a two-way table

A group of 100 students is classified as follows. Select one student uniformly at random. Let R mean that they used a revision resource and P that they passed a short quiz.

Resource use Passed Did not pass Total
Used the resource 36 24 60
Did not use the resource 14 26 40
Total 50 50 100
  1. Find \mathrm{Pr}(R\cap P) and \mathrm{Pr}(P).
  2. Find \mathrm{Pr}(P\mid R) and \mathrm{Pr}(R\mid P).
  3. Are R and P independent? Does the table establish that using the resource causes a higher chance of passing?
Solution

(a) Both probabilities use all 100 students as the denominator: \mathrm{Pr}(R\cap P)=\frac{36}{100}=0.36,\qquad \mathrm{Pr}(P)=\frac{50}{100}=0.50.

(b) Conditioning changes the group over which we calculate the proportion: \mathrm{Pr}(P\mid R)=\frac{36}{60}=0.60,\qquad \mathrm{Pr}(R\mid P)=\frac{36}{50}=0.72. Reversing the conditioning does not generally give the same probability.

(c) They are not independent because \mathrm{Pr}(P\mid R)=0.60 differs from \mathrm{Pr}(P)=0.50. Equivalently, 0.36\ne(0.60)(0.50). This is an association in the group. The table alone does not establish causation; for example, students who chose the resource might also have spent more time revising.

Important 3: Sampling without replacement

A bag contains 3 red counters and 2 blue counters. Two counters are drawn uniformly at random, one after the other, without replacement. Let R_1 and R_2 denote a red counter on the first and second draws respectively.

  1. Find \mathrm{Pr}(R_1\cap R_2).
  2. Find the probability of exactly one red counter.
  3. Use the law of total probability to find \mathrm{Pr}(R_2). Are R_1 and R_2 independent?
Solution

(a) After drawing one red counter, 2 of the remaining 4 counters are red: \mathrm{Pr}(R_1\cap R_2) =\mathrm{Pr}(R_1)\mathrm{Pr}(R_2\mid R_1) =\frac35\frac24=\frac3{10}.

(b) Red then blue and blue then red are disjoint outcomes, giving \mathrm{Pr}(\text{exactly one red}) =\frac35\frac24+\frac25\frac34=\frac35.

(c) Partitioning by the colour of the first counter, \mathrm{Pr}(R_2) =\frac35\frac24+\frac25\frac34=\frac35. The marginal red probability is the same on each draw, but \mathrm{Pr}(R_2\mid R_1)=1/2\ne3/5. The draws are dependent. Equal marginal distributions do not imply independence, and multiplying (3/5)(3/5) would give the wrong answer to part (a).

Important 4: Independent or mutually exclusive?

Roll a fair six-sided die. Define A=\{2,4,6\},\qquad B=\{1,2,3,4\},\qquad C=\{1,3,5\}.

  1. Show that A and B are independent. Are they mutually exclusive?
  2. Are A and C mutually exclusive, exhaustive, or independent?
  3. Can two events of positive probability be both independent and mutually exclusive? Justify your answer.
Solution

(a) Since A\cap B=\{2,4\}, \mathrm{Pr}(A\cap B)=\frac26=\frac13 =\frac12\frac23=\mathrm{Pr}(A)\mathrm{Pr}(B). They are independent but not mutually exclusive: both occur on outcomes 2 and 4.

(b) The events A and C are mutually exclusive and exhaustive: exactly one occurs on every roll. They are not independent because \mathrm{Pr}(A\cap C)=0\ne\frac14 =\mathrm{Pr}(A)\mathrm{Pr}(C).

(c) No. Mutual exclusivity requires an intersection of probability zero, whereas independence of two positive-probability events requires a strictly positive intersection probability. Knowing one such disjoint event occurred rules out the other.

Important 5: Total probability across three suppliers

A shop obtains 50\% of its items from supplier A, 30\% from B, and 20\% from C. Their respective defect probabilities are 0.02, 0.04, and 0.05. Select one item uniformly at random from the shop’s stock, and let D denote a defect.

  1. Find \mathrm{Pr}(D). Explain why simply averaging the three defect probabilities is inappropriate.
  2. Given that the item is defective, find the probability that it came from supplier C.
Solution

(a) Supplier identity partitions the stock, so \mathrm{Pr}(D) =(0.50)(0.02)+(0.30)(0.04)+(0.20)(0.05)=0.032. The rates need weights equal to the suppliers’ shares of the stock. An unweighted average would instead describe equal shares from all three suppliers.

(b) Bayes’ theorem gives \mathrm{Pr}(C\mid D) =\frac{\mathrm{Pr}(D\mid C)\mathrm{Pr}(C)}{\mathrm{Pr}(D)} =\frac{(0.05)(0.20)}{0.032}=\frac5{16}=0.3125. Supplier C accounts for 20\% of all items but 31.25\% of defective items.

Important 6: Bayes’ theorem and a rare fault

A fault is present in 1\% of components. An automated check flags 90\% of faulty components, but also flags 5\% of components without a fault. Let F denote a fault and A a flag.

  1. Find the probability that a component is flagged.
  2. Find \mathrm{Pr}(F\mid A). Explain why it is not 0.90.
  3. Check your answer using the expected numbers of faulty and non-faulty flagged components in a batch of 10{,}000.
Solution

(a) By total probability, \mathrm{Pr}(A)=(0.90)(0.01)+(0.05)(0.99)=0.0585.

(b) Bayes’ theorem gives \mathrm{Pr}(F\mid A)=\frac{(0.90)(0.01)}{0.0585} =\frac2{13}\approx0.1538. The value 0.90 is \mathrm{Pr}(A\mid F), the probability of a flag given a fault. Here we need the reverse conditioning. Most components have no fault, so false flags can outnumber true flags despite a relatively small false-flag rate.

(c) The expected numbers are 100 faulty and 9{,}900 non-faulty components. Of these, 90 and 495, respectively, are expected to be flagged. The ratio 90/(90+495)=2/13 agrees with part (b). These are expected counts, not a claim that every batch has exactly those counts.

Important 7: A discrete distribution from probabilities to moments

A random variable X has probability mass function p_X(x)=c(x+1),\qquad x\in\{0,1,2,3\}, and zero probability elsewhere.

  1. Find c.
  2. Write the full cumulative distribution function F_X(x).
  3. Find \mathrm{Pr}(1<X\le3) and \mathrm{Pr}(1\le X\le3).
  4. Find \mathrm{E}[X] and \mathrm{Var}(X).
Solution

(a) The probabilities must sum to one: c(1+2+3+4)=1, so c=1/10.

(b) The CDF includes all probability at or below its argument: F_X(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&x<0,\\ 0.1,&0\le x<1,\\ 0.3,&1\le x<2,\\ 0.6,&2\le x<3,\\ 1,&x\ge3. \end{cases} It is right-continuous, with a jump of size p_X(x) at each support point.

(c) We have \mathrm{Pr}(1<X\le3)=0.3+0.4=0.7, \qquad \mathrm{Pr}(1\le X\le3)=0.2+0.3+0.4=0.9. Equivalently, the first is F_X(3)-F_X(1). Including the lower endpoint adds the probability mass \mathrm{Pr}(X=1)=0.2.

(d) The first two moments are \mathrm{E}[X]=\frac{0+2+6+12}{10}=2,\qquad \mathrm{E}[X^2]=\frac{0+2+12+36}{10}=5. Thus \mathrm{Var}(X)=5-2^2=1.

Important 8: A density is not a point probability

A continuous random variable X has CDF F_X(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&x<0,\\ 4x^2,&0\le x\le\tfrac12,\\ 1,&x>\tfrac12. \end{cases}

  1. Find a probability density function for X and check that it integrates to one.
  2. Find \mathrm{Pr}(1/4<X\le1/2) and \mathrm{Pr}(X=1/4).
  3. Find f_X(1/4). Is a density value greater than one a problem?
  4. Find the median of X.
Solution

(a) Differentiating the CDF on the interior of the support gives f_X(x)=8x\quad(0<x<1/2), with density zero outside this interval. Its values at the two endpoints may be chosen arbitrarily without changing probabilities. It is non-negative and \int_0^{1/2}8x\,\mathrm{d}x=[4x^2]_0^{1/2}=1.

(b) Probabilities come from CDF differences, or areas under the density: \mathrm{Pr}(1/4<X\le1/2)=F_X(1/2)-F_X(1/4)=1-\frac14=\frac34. There is no jump at 1/4, so \mathrm{Pr}(X=1/4)=0. Changing an interval endpoint from strict to non-strict makes no difference here.

(c) The density value is f_X(1/4)=2. This is allowed: a density is a height, not a probability. Areas must lie between zero and one; individual density values need not.

(d) Solve F_X(m)=1/2: 4m^2=1/2, giving m=1/(2\sqrt2)\approx0.3536. Half the probability lies on each side of this value; the midpoint of the support need not be the median.

Important 9: Joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities

The joint probability mass function of X and Y is given below. All other pairs have probability zero.

X\backslash Y 0 1 2
0 0.10 0.20 0.10
1 0.20 0.10 0.30
  1. Find both marginal PMFs.
  2. Find \mathrm{Pr}(X=1\mid Y=2).
  3. Find the conditional PMF of Y given X=0, and hence \mathrm{E}[Y\mid X=0].
  4. Are X and Y independent?
Solution

(a) Row sums give p_X(0)=0.40 and p_X(1)=0.60. Column sums give p_Y(0)=0.30, p_Y(1)=0.30, and p_Y(2)=0.40. Each marginal PMF sums to one.

(b) Restricting to the column Y=2, \mathrm{Pr}(X=1\mid Y=2)=\frac{p_{X,Y}(1,2)}{p_Y(2)} =\frac{0.30}{0.40}=0.75.

(c) Dividing the X=0 row by its total 0.40 gives \mathrm{Pr}(Y=0\mid X=0)=0.25,\quad \mathrm{Pr}(Y=1\mid X=0)=0.50,\quad \mathrm{Pr}(Y=2\mid X=0)=0.25. Therefore \mathrm{E}[Y\mid X=0]=0(0.25)+1(0.50)+2(0.25)=1. Using the original row without normalising would not give a conditional PMF.

(d) No. For example, p_{X,Y}(0,0)=0.10\ne(0.40)(0.30)=p_X(0)p_Y(0). Independence requires this factorisation for every pair; one counterexample is enough to rule it out.

Important 10: Zero correlation does not imply independence

Let X take the values -1, 0, and 1, each with probability 1/3, and define Y=X^2.

  1. Find \mathrm{E}[X], \mathrm{E}[Y], and \mathrm{E}[XY].
  2. Find the covariance and correlation of X and Y. You may use \mathrm{Var}(X)=2/3 and \mathrm{Var}(Y)=2/9.
  3. Show that X and Y are nevertheless dependent.
Solution

(a) Direct summation gives \mathrm{E}[X]=\frac{-1+0+1}{3}=0,\qquad \mathrm{E}[Y]=\frac{1+0+1}{3}=\frac23. Since XY=X^3, \mathrm{E}[XY]=(-1+0+1)/3=0.

(b) Thus \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathrm{E}[XY]-\mathrm{E}[X]\mathrm{E}[Y]=0. Both variances are positive, so the correlation is defined and equals zero.

(c) If X=0, then Y=0 with certainty, whereas \mathrm{Pr}(Y=0)=1/3: \mathrm{Pr}(Y=0\mid X=0)=1\ne\mathrm{Pr}(Y=0). Zero correlation excludes a linear association as measured by covariance; it does not exclude a nonlinear relationship such as Y=X^2.

Important 11: Counting events with indicator variables

Four components have fault probabilities p_1=0.1, p_2=0.2, p_3=0.3, and p_4=0.4. Define I_i=1 if component i is faulty and I_i=0 otherwise. Let N=I_1+I_2+I_3+I_4 be the number of faulty components.

  1. Find \mathrm{E}[N] without assuming independence.
  2. Now assume the fault indicators are independent. Find \mathrm{Var}(N).
  3. Explain why N is not Binomial with parameters 4 and 0.25, even though the average fault probability is 0.25.
  4. Without independence, what additional information would be needed to find \mathrm{Var}(N)?
Solution

(a) Each I_i is Bernoulli with mean p_i. Linearity of expectation gives \mathrm{E}[N]=\sum_{i=1}^4p_i=1. This calculation does not need independence.

(b) Under independence, the covariances vanish, so \mathrm{Var}(N)=\sum_{i=1}^4p_i(1-p_i) =0.09+0.16+0.21+0.24=0.70.

(c) A Binomial model assumes a common success probability as well as independent trials. Here the probabilities differ. In particular, a \mathrm{Binomial}(4,0.25) variable would have variance 4(0.25)(0.75)=0.75, which differs from part (b).

(d) The pairwise covariances, or equivalent joint fault probabilities, are needed because in general \mathrm{Var}(N)=\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)+2\sum_{i<j}\mathrm{Cov}(I_i,I_j). The four marginal fault probabilities alone are insufficient.

Important 12: One observation versus a sample mean

Let X_1,\ldots,X_{25} be independent \mathcal N(50,100) measurements, where the second Normal parameter is the variance. Let \bar X be their mean. You may use \Phi(0.4)=0.6554 and \Phi(2)=0.9772, where \Phi is the standard Normal CDF.

  1. State the standard deviations of X_1 and \bar X.
  2. Find \mathrm{Pr}(X_1>54) and \mathrm{Pr}(\bar X>54).
  3. Is the Normal distribution of \bar X exact or a CLT approximation here?
Solution

(a) One measurement has standard deviation \sqrt{100}=10. Independence gives \mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=100/25=4, so the standard deviation of the mean (its standard error) is 2.

(b) Standardise with the standard deviation of the quantity in question: \mathrm{Pr}(X_1>54)=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{54-50}{10}\right) =1-0.6554=0.3446, whereas \mathrm{Pr}(\bar X>54)=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{54-50}{2}\right) =1-0.9772=0.0228. Averaging reduces variability, so the same distance above the mean is much less likely for \bar X.

(c) It is exact: a linear combination of independent Normal random variables is Normal, so \bar X\sim\mathcal N(50,4). A large-sample approximation is unnecessary under this model.

ImportantNewspaper subscriptions

In a certain city, 50\% of families subscribe to the morning newspaper, 65\% subscribe to the afternoon newspaper, and 30\% subscribe to both.

What proportion of families subscribe to at least one newspaper?

Solution

Let M = “morning” and A = “afternoon”.

By the addition rule of probability:

\mathrm{Pr}(M \cup A) \;=\; \mathrm{Pr}(M) + \mathrm{Pr}(A) - \mathrm{Pr}(M \cap A).

Substituting:

0.5 + 0.65 - 0.3 \;=\; 0.85.

So, 85\% of families subscribe to at least one newspaper.

Let X_1,\dots,X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu,1). Find the MLE of \mu.

Solution

The likelihood is

L(\mu ; \underline{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(x_i - \mu)^2\right) = (2\pi)^{-n/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 \right) .

Taking logs:

\ell(\mu) = -\tfrac{n}{2}\log(2\pi) - \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2.

Differentiate with respect to \mu:

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} = \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu).

Setting equal to 0:

\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\mu_{\mathrm{obs}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = \bar x.

So the MLE is the sample-mean estimator \bar X; for these observed data its value is \bar x.

ImportantMinimiser of \mathrm{E}[(X-c)^2]

Suppose that X is a continuous random variable. Show that \mathrm{E}[(X-c)^2] is minimised when c is the population mean. State any assumptions.

Solution

As X is continuous, \mathrm{E}[(X-c)^2] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty (x-c)^2 f(x)\,dx.

We assume we can interchange differentiation with respect to c and integration with respect to x. Then \frac{\partial}{\partial c}\,\mathrm{E}[(X-c)^2] = \mathrm{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial c}(X-c)^2\right] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{\partial}{\partial c}(x-c)^2 f(x)\,dx.

Differentiating: \frac{\partial}{\partial c}\,\mathrm{E}[(X-c)^2] = -2\int_{-\infty}^\infty (x-c) f(x)\,dx = -2\{\mathrm{E}[X] - c\}.

This derivative is zero when c = \mathrm{E}[X].

The second derivative is positive, so this is indeed a minimum.

Suppose that X is a continuous random variable. Show that \mathrm{E}[|X-c|] is minimised when c is the population median. State any assumptions.

Solution

We assume the technical conditions allowing us to interchange differentiation with respect to c and integration with respect to x.

Using the hint: \frac{\partial}{\partial c}\,\mathrm{E}[|X-c|] = \mathrm{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial c}|X-c|\right] = \mathrm{E}[I(X \leq c) - I(X > c)], where I(\cdot) is the indicator function.

So, \frac{\partial}{\partial c}\,\mathrm{E}[|X-c|] = \mathrm{Pr}(X \leq c) - \mathrm{Pr}(X > c).

Since \mathrm{Pr}(X > c) = 1 - \mathrm{Pr}(X \leq c), this simplifies to \frac{\partial}{\partial c}\,\mathrm{E}[|X-c|] = 2\mathrm{Pr}(X \leq c) - 1.

This derivative is zero when \mathrm{Pr}(X \leq c) = \tfrac{1}{2}, i.e. when c is the median of the distribution of X.

ImportantDistribution of a Squared Normal Variable

Solution

For y > 0: F_Y(y) = \mathrm{Pr}(Y \leq y) = \mathrm{Pr}(Z^2 \leq y).

This is equivalent to F_Y(y) = \mathrm{Pr}(-\sqrt{y} \leq Z \leq \sqrt{y}) = \mathrm{Pr}(Z \leq \sqrt{y}) - \mathrm{Pr}(Z \leq -\sqrt{y}).

Differentiate to find the PDF:

f_Y(y) = \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \Big[ \Phi(\sqrt{y}) - \Phi(-\sqrt{y}) \Big].

ImportantExact Binomial Confidence Interval (Clopper–Pearson)

Suppose we have a single Binomial Binomial observation

X \mid p \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n,p).

Following the general strategy General Recipe, construct an exact two-sided (1-\alpha) confidence interval for p.

Note on exact (discrete) coverage. In the discrete case, an “exact” interval [L(X), U(X)] means, for every 0\leq p \leq 1,

\operatorname{Pr}\!\big(L(X) \leq p \leq U(X)\big) \ge 1-\alpha, \quad\text{where }X \sim \text{Binomial}(n,p).

Because of discreteness, equality for all p is impossible; the interval is conservative.

Solution

1. Choose a statistic. Take T=X. Its sampling distribution is

X \mid p \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n,p),\qquad f(x\mid p)=\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}.

2–3. Form a pivot via tail probabilities and fix central mass. Denote F_p(y) = \mathrm{Pr}(Y \leq y) where Y\sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n,p) and denote \mathrm{Pr}_{p}(X\le x) = F_p(x). For a given observed x, define endpoints by equating the two tails to \alpha/2:

\mathrm{Pr}_{p_L}(X\le x-1)=\frac{\alpha}{2},\qquad \mathrm{Pr}_{p_U}(X\ge x+1)=\frac{\alpha}{2}.

(Edge cases: if x=0 set p_L=0; if x=n set p_U=1.)

4. Invert (Clopper–Pearson). These tail equalities are exactly equivalent to Beta quantiles, giving closed-form endpoints:

\boxed{\; p_L = B^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{\alpha}{2};\, x,\; n-x+1\right),\qquad p_U = B^{-1}\!\left(1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2};\, x+1,\; n-x\right)\; }

where B^{-1}(q;a,b) is the q-quantile of \mathrm{Beta}(a,b). With the edge-case conventions above:

p_L= \begin{cases} 0, & x=0,\\ B^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{\alpha}{2};\, x,\; n-x+1\right), & x\ge 1, \end{cases} \quad p_U= \begin{cases} B^{-1}\!\left(1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2};\, x+1,\; n-x\right), & x\le n-1,\\ 1, & x=n. \end{cases}

We obtain the Clopper–Pearson interval [p_L,\,p_U], which is an exact (conservative) two-sided (1-\alpha) CI for p.

ImportantProbability Integral Transform

Suppose X is a continuous random variable with CDF F(x).
Define the random variable U = F(X), that is, the CDF evaluated at a random argument.

Show that U \sim U(0,1).

Aside. This result is fundamental in simulation: if we can generate uniform random variables U \sim U(0,1), then we can simulate X by setting X = F^{-1}(U).

Because F(\cdot) is continuous and strictly increasing, its inverse F^{-1}(\cdot) exists and is also strictly increasing.

Define the CDF of U as G(u) = \operatorname{Pr}(U \leq u).

Since U = F(X) always lies in [0,1], we have: G(u) = 0 \quad \text{for } u \leq 0, \qquad G(u) = 1 \quad \text{for } u \geq 1.

ImportantMoment Generating Function of the Exponential

We have seen that properties of a random variable X can be described by the CDF, PMF, or PDF.
Another way is through the moment generating function (MGF) M(t), defined by M(t) = \mathrm{E}[e^{tX}], provided the expectation is finite (which is not always the case).

If the MGF exists, it is unique: two random variables have the same MGF if and only if they have the same distribution.

Solution

Combine exponents: M(t) = \int_0^\infty \lambda e^{-(\lambda - t)x} \, dx.

Factor out (\lambda - t): M(t) = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - t} \int_0^\infty (\lambda - t) e^{-(\lambda - t)x} \, dx.

But the integrand is the PDF of \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda - t), so the integral equals 1.

Therefore: M(t) = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda - t}, \qquad 0 \leq t < \lambda.

Important 14: Estimating \lambda from Independent Poisson Samples

Suppose X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda) and, independently, Y \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(2\lambda).

  1. Classify the following as parameters, statistics, or neither:

    1. X + 2Y
    2. \hat{\lambda} = (X+Y)/3
    3. \lambda / \hat{\lambda}
    4. \operatorname{Pr}(\lambda > \hat{\lambda})

  2. Find the bias and MSE of each of the following estimators of \lambda. Which would you recommend?

    1. X
    2. Y-X
    3. (X+Y)/3

  3. Consider a general estimator w_1 X + w_2 Y. What values of w_1 and w_2 would you recommend?

Solution

a. Classification.

  • A statistic is a function of data (random variables).
  • A parameter depends only on the population.
  • Neither means it involves both.
  1. X+2Y: Statistic
  2. (X+Y)/3: Statistic
  3. \lambda/\hat{\lambda}: Neither
  4. \operatorname{Pr}(\lambda > \hat{\lambda}): Parameter (depends on the distribution, not directly observable)

b. Bias and MSE.

Recall: for Poisson(\mu), \mathrm{E}[X]=\mathrm{Var}(X)=\mu.

  • X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda), so \mathrm{E}[X]=\lambda, \mathrm{Var}(X)=\lambda.
  • Y \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(2\lambda), so \mathrm{E}[Y]=2\lambda, \mathrm{Var}(Y)=2\lambda.
  • Independence: \mathrm{Var}(aX+bY) = a^2\mathrm{Var}(X) + b^2\mathrm{Var}(Y).

\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \lambda, \qquad \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2.

  1. X:
    \mathrm{E}[X] = \lambda \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Bias}=0.
    \mathrm{MSE} = \mathrm{Var}(X) = \lambda.

  2. Y-X:
    \mathrm{E}[Y-X] = 2\lambda - \lambda = \lambda \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Bias}=0.
    \mathrm{Var}(Y-X) = \mathrm{Var}(Y)+\mathrm{Var}(X) = 2\lambda + \lambda = 3\lambda.
    \mathrm{MSE}=3\lambda.

  3. (X+Y)/3:
    \mathrm{E}[(X+Y)/3] = (\lambda+2\lambda)/3 = \lambda \;\Rightarrow\; \text{Bias}=0.
    \mathrm{Var}[(X+Y)/3] = \tfrac{1}{9}(\lambda+2\lambda) = \tfrac{\lambda}{3}.
    \mathrm{MSE} = \tfrac{\lambda}{3}.

Recommendation: (X+Y)/3 has the smallest MSE.

c. General estimator w_1X + w_2Y.

Expectation: \mathrm{E}[w_1X+w_2Y] = (w_1 + 2w_2)\lambda.

For unbiasedness, require w_1+2w_2=1, i.e. w_1=1-2w_2.

Variance:

\begin{align*} \mathrm{Var}((1-2w_2)X + w_2Y) &= (1-2w_2)^2\lambda + 2w_2^2\lambda \\ &= (6w_2^2 - 4w_2 + 1)\lambda. \end{align*}

This quadratic in w_2 is minimised at w_2=\tfrac{1}{3}, giving w_1=\tfrac{1}{3}.

Thus the optimal unbiased estimator is: \hat{\lambda} = \tfrac{1}{3}(X+Y), matching part (b).

\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta, \qquad \text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2.

Using independence of X and Y:

\mathrm{Var}(aX+bY+c) = a^2\mathrm{Var}(X) + b^2\mathrm{Var}(Y).

Important 15: Properties of Poisson Estimators

Suppose that X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n, for n \geq 1, are independent \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda) random variables.

Two possible estimators of \lambda are: \hat{\lambda}_1 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \qquad \hat{\lambda}_2 = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i.

  1. Show that \hat{\lambda}_1 is unbiased but \hat{\lambda}_2 is not.

  2. Show that \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_1) = \frac{\lambda}{n}, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_2) = \frac{\lambda}{n^3}.

  3. For each estimator, determine whether it can be shown to be consistent using its mean and variance.

Solution

a. Unbiasedness.

\mathrm{E}[\hat{\lambda}_1] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[X_i] = \frac{1}{n} \cdot n\lambda = \lambda.

So \hat{\lambda}_1 is unbiased.

\mathrm{E}[\hat{\lambda}_2] = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[X_i] = \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot n\lambda = \frac{\lambda}{n}.

So \hat{\lambda}_2 is biased.

b. Variances.

Since X_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda), \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \lambda.

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_1) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot n\lambda = \frac{\lambda}{n}.

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_2) = \frac{1}{n^4}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{1}{n^4} \cdot n\lambda = \frac{\lambda}{n^3}.

c. Consistency.

  • \hat{\lambda}_1: unbiased and \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\lambda}_1)\to 0 as n\to\infty, so \hat{\lambda}_1 is consistent.
  • \hat{\lambda}_2: \mathrm{E}[\hat{\lambda}_2] = \lambda/n \to 0 as n\to\infty, so it does not converge to \lambda. Therefore it is not consistent.

ImportantDeriving the Student-t distribution

Let X_1,\ldots, X_n \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2). From Proposition 4.2 and Proposition 4.3, we saw that

  1. \bar{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n),
  2. \dfrac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \;\sim\; \chi^2_{\,n-1}, and
  3. The sample variance S^2 was independent of the sample mean \bar{X}.

Derive the PDF of the random variable T = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{S / \sqrt{n}}.

Solution

Set Z \;=\; \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1), \qquad U \;=\; \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{\,n-1}, with Z independent of U. Then T \;=\; \frac{\sigma Z}{\sqrt{(\sigma^2 U)/(n-1)}} \;=\; \frac{Z}{\sqrt{U/\nu}}, \qquad \nu := n-1.

Conditional distribution. Conditioning on U=u, T \mid U=u \;=\; \frac{Z}{\sqrt{u/\nu}} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; \frac{\nu}{u}\right), so its conditional density is f_{T\mid U}(t\mid u) = \sqrt{\frac{u}{2\pi\nu}}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{u}{2\nu}t^2\right), \qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Mixture over U. The marginal PDF of T is f_T(t) = \int_0^\infty f_{T\mid U}(t\mid u)\, f_U(u)\, \mathrm{d}u,

where f_U(u) = \dfrac{1}{2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)}\,u^{\nu/2-1} e^{-u/2} for u>0. Thus

\begin{align*} f_T(t) &= \int_0^\infty \left[\sqrt{\frac{u}{2\pi\nu}}\;e^{-\frac{u}{2\nu}t^2}\right] \left[\frac{1}{2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)}\,u^{\nu/2-1} e^{-u/2}\right] \mathrm{d}u \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\nu}\,2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)} \int_0^\infty u^{\frac{\nu+1}{2}-1} \exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\!\left(1+\frac{t^2}{\nu}\right)u\right\} \,\mathrm{d}u. \end{align*}

Use the Gamma integral \displaystyle \int_0^\infty u^{a-1} e^{-b u}\,\mathrm{d}u = \Gamma(a)\,b^{-a} with a=\frac{\nu+1}{2} and b=\frac{1}{2}\!\left(1+\frac{t^2}{\nu}\right):

\begin{align*} f_T(t) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\nu}\,2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)} \cdot \Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right) \left[\frac{1}{2}\!\left(1+\frac{t^2}{\nu}\right)\right]^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}} \\ &= \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)} \left(1+\frac{t^2}{\nu}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}}, \qquad t\in\mathbb{R}. \end{align*}

This is the Student-t density with \nu=n-1 degrees of freedom: T \sim t_\nu \quad \text{with} \quad f_T(t) = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)} \left(1+\frac{t^2}{\nu}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}}.

Important 18: Normal Approximation to the Binomial

Use the Central Limit Theorem 4.1 to justify the Normal approximation to the Binomial Binomial.

That is, if X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n,p), show that for large n (with p \neq 0,1), X \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N} \big(np,\; np(1-p)\big).

Solution

The Binomial distribution counts the number of successes in n independent Bernoulli trials.

For trial i, define the indicator I_i = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if success}, \\ 0, & \text{if failure}. \end{cases}

Each I_i has \mathrm{E}[I_i] = p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(I_i) = p(1-p).

Important 23: Proving Consistency I

Let X_1,\dots,X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathrm{Distribution}(\mu, \sigma^2) be a random sample such that each \operatorname{E}[X_i]=\mu,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2. Consider the following family of estimators of the mean \mu: \hat{\mu}_n = \frac{1}{n + c} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i, where c\in\mathbb{R} is a constant.

Show that \hat{\mu}_n is a consistent estimator of \mu for all c\in\mathbb{R}.

Solution

Bias: We have

\begin{align*} \mathrm{E}[\hat{\mu}_n] &= \mathrm{E}\left[\frac{1}{n + c} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right] \\ &= \frac{1}{n+c} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[X_i] \\ &= \frac{1}{n+c} \sum_{i=1}^n \mu \\ &= \frac{n \mu }{n + c}. \end{align*}

Therefore, the bias is \mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\mu}_n) = \frac{n \mu }{n + c} - \mu = \frac{n\mu}{n+c} - \frac{(n+c)\mu}{n+c} = -\frac{c \mu}{n + c}.

Variance: Due to independence of the X_i, we have

\begin{align*} \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\mu}_n] &= \mathrm{Var}\left[\frac{1}{n + c} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right] \\ &= \frac{1}{(n+c)^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}[X_i] \\ &= \frac{1}{(n+c)^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma^2 \\ &= \frac{n \sigma^2 }{(n + c)^2}. \end{align*}

Consistency: To show consistency, we use Proposition 4.5. From the computed bias, we have (for any value of c\in\mathbb{R}): \mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\mu}_n) = -\frac{c \mu}{n + c} \rightarrow 0, \quad \text{as } n\rightarrow \infty. Therefore, the estimator is asymptotically unbiased. Similarly, the variance converges to 0 for all c\in\mathbb{R}: \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mu}_n) = \frac{n \sigma^2 }{(n + c)^2} \rightarrow 0, \quad \text{as } n\rightarrow \infty. Therefore, \hat{\mu}_n is a consistent estimator of \mu for all c\in\mathbb{R}.

Important 24: Computing Likelihoods I

Let X_1,\dots,X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathrm{Geometric}(p) be a random sample.

Derive the likelihood function.

Solution For independent and identically distributed data, the likelihood function 5.1 takes the form L(p ; \underline{x}) = f(\underline{x} \mid p) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid p), where f(x_i\mid p) is PMF of the geometric distribution Geometric: f(x_i \mid p) = p(1-p)^{x_i - 1}. Therefore, the likelihood takes the form \begin{align*} L(p; \underline{x}) &= \prod_{i=1}^n p(1-p)^{x_i - 1} \\ &= p(1-p)^{x_1-1}\times p(1-p)^{x_2-1}\times \ldots \times p(1-p)^{x_n-1} \\ &= p^n(1-p)^{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - 1)} \\ &= p^n(1-p)^{n(\bar{x} - 1)}, \end{align*} where \bar{x} is the sample mean 4.1.
Important 25: Computing Likelihoods II

Let X_1,\dots,X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1) be a random sample.

Derive the likelihood function.

Solution For independent and identically distributed data, the likelihood function 5.1 takes the form L(\mu ; \underline{x}) = f(\underline{x} \mid \mu) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \mu), where f(x_i\mid \mu) is PDF of the normal distribution Normal (with variance \sigma^2=1): f(x_i \mid \mu) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{2} \right\}. Therefore, the likelihood takes the form \begin{align*} L(\mu; \underline{x}) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{2} \right\} \\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(x_1 - \mu)^2}{2} \right\} \times \ldots \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left\{ -\frac{(x_n - \mu)^2}{2} \right\} \\ &= (2\pi)^{-n/2} \exp\left\{ \sum_{i=1}^n -\frac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{2} \right\} \\ &= (2\pi)^{-n/2} \exp\left\{ -\frac{1}{2 }\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 \right\}. \end{align*}
Important 26: Computing Likelihoods III

Suppose we have n i.i.d. log normal Lognormal observations X_1,\dots,X_n \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathrm{LogNormal}(\mu, \gamma).

Derive the likelihood function.

Solution

For independent and identically distributed data, the likelihood function 5.1 takes the form L(\mu, \gamma ; \underline{x}) = f(\underline{x} \mid \mu, \gamma) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \mu, \gamma), where f(x_i\mid \mu, \gamma) is PDF of the log normal distribution Lognormal (with \sigma^2 = \gamma): f(x_i \mid \mu, \gamma) = \frac{1}{x_i\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\log(x_i)-\mu)^2}{2\gamma}\right). Therefore, the likelihood takes the form \begin{align*} L(\mu, \gamma; \underline{x}) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{x_i\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\log(x_i)-\mu)^2}{2\gamma}\right) \\ &= (2\pi\gamma)^{-n/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^n (\log(x_i) - \mu)^2\right\} \left(\prod_{i=1}^n x_i\right)^{-1}. \end{align*}

Important 27: Fisher Information for a Binomial Observation

Suppose we have a single Binomial Binomial observation: X \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, \theta), Derive the expected Fisher information 5.7 \mathcal I(\theta) and the observed information 5.10 j(\theta;x). Evaluate both at the MLE when 0<x<n.

Solution

We compute \mathcal I(\theta)=-\mathrm E_\theta[\ell''(\theta;X)] and j(\theta;x)=-\ell''(\theta;x). The former averages over possible data; the latter is the curvature of the realised log-likelihood.

Step 1: Compute log-likelihood

Since we have a single observation, the likelihood is just the Binomial PMF: L(\theta ; x) = \binom{n}{x} \theta^x(1-\theta)^{n-x}. Therefore, the log-likelihood takes the form \ell(\theta; x) = \log L(\theta ; x) = \log\left(\binom{n}{x}\right) + x \log \theta + (n-x)\log(1-\theta).

Step 2: Compute Derivatives

We compute the first and second derivatives of the log-likelihood with respect to \theta: \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \ell(\theta; x) &= \frac{x}{\theta} - \frac{n-x}{1-\theta} \\ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} \ell(\theta; x) &= -\frac{x}{\theta^2} - \frac{n-x}{(1-\theta)^2}. \end{align*}

Step 3: Compute Fisher Information

We have \mathcal{I}(\theta) = -\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2} \ell(\theta; X)\right] = -\mathrm{E}\left[-\frac{X}{\theta^2} - \frac{n-X}{(1-\theta)^2}\right], where the random component of the expectation is X\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n, \theta). Therefore, manipulating the expectation: \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}(\theta) &= \mathrm{E}\left[\frac{X}{\theta^2} + \frac{n-X}{(1-\theta)^2}\right] \\ &= \frac{1}{\theta^2}\mathrm{E}[X] + \frac{1}{(1-\theta)^2} \mathrm{E}[n - X] \\ &= \frac{n\theta}{\theta^2} + \frac{n - n\theta}{(1-\theta)^2}, \end{align*} using the fact that \mathrm{E}[X] = n\theta. Continuing: \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}(\theta) &= \frac{n}{\theta} + \frac{n}{1-\theta} \\ &= \frac{n(1-\theta) + n\theta}{\theta(1-\theta)} \\ &= \frac{n}{\theta(1-\theta)}. \end{align*}

The observed information is therefore j(\theta;x)=\frac{x}{\theta^2}+\frac{n-x}{(1-\theta)^2}.

Step 4: Compute MLE

For 0<x<n, the interior MLE solves: \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \ell(\theta; x) &= 0 \\ \frac{x}{\theta} - \frac{n-x}{1-\theta} &= 0 \\ x(1-\theta) &= \theta(n-x) \\ x &= n\theta \\ \Rightarrow \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}} &= \frac{x}{n}. \end{align*}

Step 5: Evaluate both information measures at the MLE

The plug-in expected information is \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}}) &= \frac{n}{\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}}(1-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}})} \\ &= \frac{n}{x/n(1-x/n)} \\ &= \frac{n^3}{x(n - x)}. \end{align*} In this model the observed information happens to agree at the MLE: j(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}};x) =\frac{x}{(x/n)^2}+\frac{n-x}{(1-x/n)^2} =\frac{n^3}{x(n-x)}. This equality is model-specific; the two definitions should not be conflated.

Important 28: Fisher Information for Geometric Data

Suppose we have a random Geometric Geometric sample: X_1,\ldots, X_n \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathrm{Geom}(\theta). Derive the expected Fisher information 5.7 for one observation and for the whole sample. Also derive the observed information 5.10 for the realised sample and evaluate it at the MLE.

Solution

Since the observations are i.i.d., expected information is additive: \mathcal I_n(\theta)=n\mathcal I_1(\theta). We first work with the whole sample, then retain the realised curvature for the observed information.

Step 1: Compute log-likelihood

For i.i.d. Geometric data, we have \begin{align*} L(\theta; \underline{x}) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \theta(1-\theta)^{x_i - 1} \\ &= \theta^n(1-\theta)^{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - 1)} \\ &= \theta^n(1-\theta)^{n(\bar{x} - 1)}. \end{align*} Therefore, the log-likelihood takes the form \ell(\theta; \underline{x}) = \log L(\theta ; \underline{x}) = n\log(\theta) + n(\bar{x}-1)\log(1-\theta).

Step 2: Compute Derivatives

We compute the first and second derivatives of the log-likelihood with respect to \theta: \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \ell(\theta; \underline{x}) &= \frac{n}{\theta} - \frac{n(\bar{x} - 1)}{1-\theta} \\ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} \ell(\theta; \underline{x}) &= -\frac{n}{\theta^2} - \frac{n(\bar{x} - 1)}{(1-\theta)^2}. \end{align*}

Step 3: Compute Fisher Information

We have \mathcal{I}_n(\theta) = -\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2} \ell(\theta; \underline{X})\right] = -\mathrm{E}\left[-\frac{n}{\theta^2} - \frac{n(\bar{X} - 1)}{(1-\theta)^2}\right], where the random component of the expectation is X_i\sim\mathrm{Geom}(\theta). Therefore, manipulating the expectation: \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}_n(\theta) &= \mathrm{E}\left[\frac{n}{\theta^2} + \frac{n(\bar{X} - 1)}{(1-\theta)^2}\right] \\ &= \frac{n}{\theta^2} + \frac{n}{(1-\theta)^2} \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}] - \frac{n}{(1-\theta)^2} \\ &= \frac{n}{\theta^2} + \frac{n}{(1-\theta)^2} \frac{1}{\theta} - \frac{n}{(1-\theta)^2}, \end{align*} where we used the fact that \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[X_i] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\theta} = \frac{1}{\theta}, and \mathrm{E}[X_i] = \tfrac{1}{\theta}. Continuing: \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}_n(\theta) &= \frac{n (1-\theta)^2}{\theta^2(1-\theta)^2} + \frac{n\theta}{\theta^2(1-\theta)^2} - \frac{n\theta^2}{\theta^2(1-\theta)^2}\\ &= \frac{n(1-\theta) +n\theta}{\theta^2(1-\theta)} \\ &= \frac{n}{\theta^2(1-\theta)}. \end{align*} This immediately implies the Fisher information for a single observation is \mathcal{I}_1(\theta) = \frac{1}{\theta^2(1-\theta)}.

For the realised sample, the observed information is j_n(\theta;\underline x) =-\ell''(\theta;\underline x) =\frac{n}{\theta^2} +\frac{n(\bar x-1)}{(1-\theta)^2}. For one realised observation x, this reduces to j_1(\theta;x)=\theta^{-2}+(x-1)(1-\theta)^{-2}.

Step 4: Compute MLE

The interior MLE solves: \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \ell(\theta; \underline{x}) &= 0 \\ \frac{n}{\theta} - \frac{n(\bar{x} - 1)}{1-\theta} &= 0 \\ n(1-\theta) &= n \theta (\bar{x} - 1) \\ 1 &= \theta \bar{x} \\ \Rightarrow \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}} &= \frac{1}{\bar{x}}. \end{align*}

Step 5: Evaluate the information at the MLE

The plug-in expected information is \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}_n(\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}}) &= \frac{n}{\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}}^2(1-\hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MLE}})} \\ &= \frac{n}{\bar{x}^{-2}(1-\bar{x}^{-1})}. \\ &= \frac{n \bar{x}^3}{\bar{x} - 1}. \end{align*} Substitution in the realised curvature gives the same value here: j_n(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MLE}};\underline x) =\frac{n\bar x^3}{\bar x-1}. The corresponding average information per observation is this quantity divided by n; it is not the observed information from one separately observed Geometric variable.

Important 29: Fisher Information of the Variance of Normal Data

Suppose we have a random Normal Normal sample: X_1,\ldots, X_n \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\mu, \gamma), where \mu is a known value and \gamma is unknown.

Derive the expected Fisher information 5.7 and the observed information 5.10 for \gamma using the whole random sample. Evaluate both at the MLE.

Solution

We compute \mathcal I_n(\gamma)=-\mathrm E_\gamma[\ell''(\gamma;\underline X)] and j_n(\gamma;\underline x)=-\ell''(\gamma;\underline x) separately.

Step 1: Compute log-likelihood

Since we have n i.i.d. \mathcal{N}(\mu, \gamma) with \mu known, the likelihood function is \begin{align*} L(\gamma;\underline{x}) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}} \exp\left\{-\frac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{2\gamma}\right\} \\ &= (2 \pi)^{-n/2} \gamma^{-n/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2\right\}. \end{align*} Therefore, the log-likelihood takes the form \ell(\gamma; \underline{x}) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\log(\gamma) - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2.

Step 2: Compute Derivatives

We compute the first and second derivatives of the log-likelihood with respect to \gamma: \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \gamma} \ell(\gamma; \underline{x}) &= -\frac{n}{2\gamma} + \frac{1}{2\gamma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2, \\ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \gamma^2} \ell(\gamma; \underline{x}) &= \frac{n}{2\gamma^2} - \frac{1}{\gamma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2. \end{align*} Step 3: Compute Fisher Information

We have \mathcal{I}(\gamma) = -\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\gamma^2} \ell(\gamma; \underline{X})\right] = -\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{n}{2\gamma^2} - \frac{1}{\gamma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2\right], where the random component of the expectation is the random data X_i\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu, \gamma). Therefore, manipulating the expectation: \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}(\gamma) &= -\frac{n}{2\gamma^2} + \frac{1}{\gamma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[(X_i - \mu)^2] \\ &= -\frac{n}{2\gamma^2} + \frac{1}{\gamma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n \gamma, \end{align*} using the fact that each X_i - \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \gamma) and so \mathrm{E}[(X_i - \mu)^2] = \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \gamma. Continuing: \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}(\gamma) &= -\frac{n}{2\gamma^2} + \frac{n\gamma}{\gamma^3} \\ &= \frac{n}{2\gamma^2} \end{align*}

For the realised data, the observed information is j_n(\gamma;\underline x) =-\frac{n}{2\gamma^2} +\frac{1}{\gamma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)^2.

Step 4: Compute MLE

The interior MLE solves: \begin{align*} \frac{\partial}{\partial \gamma} \ell(\gamma; \underline{x}) &= 0 \\ -\frac{n}{2\gamma} + \frac{1}{2\gamma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 &= 0 \\ \Rightarrow \hat{\gamma}_{\mathrm{MLE}} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 \end{align*}

Step 5: Evaluate the information at the MLE

The plug-in expected information is \begin{align*} \mathcal{I}(\hat{\gamma}_{\mathrm{MLE}}) &= \frac{n}{2\hat{\gamma}_{\mathrm{MLE}}^2} \\ &= \frac{n^3}{2\left(\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2\right)^2}. \end{align*} Because \sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2=n\hat\gamma_{\mathrm{MLE}}, evaluating the observed information also gives j_n(\hat\gamma_{\mathrm{MLE}};\underline x) =-\frac{n}{2\hat\gamma_{\mathrm{MLE}}^2} +\frac{n\hat\gamma_{\mathrm{MLE}}}{\hat\gamma_{\mathrm{MLE}}^3} =\frac{n}{2\hat\gamma_{\mathrm{MLE}}^2}.

Important 30: Asymptotic Posterior for the Variance Parameter

Determine the asymptotic posterior distribution for \gamma.

Solution

Step 1: Likelihood

f(\underline{x} \mid \gamma) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \gamma}} \exp\!\left[-\frac{x_i^2}{2\gamma}\right] = (2\pi \gamma)^{-n/2} \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\right].

Step 2: Log-likelihood

\ell(\gamma; \underline{x}) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\log(\gamma) - \frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2.

Step 3: Derivatives

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \gamma} = -\frac{n}{2\gamma} + \frac{1}{2\gamma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2,

\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \gamma^2} = \frac{n}{2\gamma^2} - \frac{1}{\gamma^3}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2.

Let
a = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2.

Step 4: MLE

Solving \partial \ell / \partial \gamma = 0:

-\frac{n}{2\gamma} + \frac{a}{2\gamma^2} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat{\gamma} = \frac{a}{n} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2.

Step 5: Fisher Information

For one observation:

\ell(\gamma) = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\gamma) - \tfrac{x^2}{2\gamma}, and so \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \gamma^2} = \frac{1}{2\gamma^2} - \frac{x^2}{\gamma^3}.

Taking expectation under X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\gamma):

\mathcal{I}(\gamma) = -\mathrm{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \gamma^2}\right] = -\left(\frac{1}{2\gamma^2} - \frac{\gamma}{\gamma^3}\right) = \frac{1}{2\gamma^2}.

Thus for n observations:

\mathcal{I}_n(\gamma) = \frac{n}{2\gamma^2}.

Evaluated at the MLE:

\mathcal{I}_n(\hat{\gamma}) = \frac{n}{2\hat{\gamma}^2}.

Step 6: Asymptotic posterior

By Theorem 6.2

\gamma \mid \underline{x} \approx \mathcal{N}\!\left( \hat{\gamma},\; I_n(\hat{\gamma})^{-1} \right) = \mathcal{N}\!\left( \hat{\gamma},\; \frac{2\hat{\gamma}^2}{n} \right).

That is,

\boxed{ \gamma \mid \underline{x} \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\!\left( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2,\; \frac{2}{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\right)^2 \right). }

  1. Determine the general asymptotic posterior distribution 6.2 for \lambda.
  2. Suppose we observe n = 100 data points with sample mean \bar{x} = 3.21. Compute the asymptotic posterior distribution.

Important 32: Asymptotic Posterior for Exponential Data

Suppose we have independent Exponential observations with rate parameter \lambda: X_1,\ldots,X_n \mid \lambda \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda).

  1. Determine the general asymptotic posterior distribution 6.2 for \lambda.
  2. Suppose we observe n = 200 data points with sample mean \bar{x} = 7.14. Compute the asymptotic posterior distribution.
Solution

  1. General Asymptotic Posterior

    1. Likelihood and log-likelihood

    The density of each X_i is f(x_i \mid \lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x_i}.

    Thus the likelihood is f(\underline{x} \mid \lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda e^{-\lambda x_i} = \lambda^n \exp\!\left(-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\right).

    The log-likelihood is \ell(\lambda; \underline{x}) = n\log\lambda - \lambda\sum_{i=1}^n x_i = n\log\lambda - n\lambda \bar{x}.

    1. Derivatives

    \frac{\partial}{\partial \lambda} \ell(\lambda; \underline{x}) = \frac{n}{\lambda} - n\bar{x}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \lambda^2} \ell(\lambda; \underline{x}) = -\frac{n}{\lambda^2}.

    1. MLE

    Solve \frac{n}{\lambda} - n\bar{x} = 0, which gives the maximum likelihood estimator \hat{\lambda} = \frac{1}{\bar{X}}.

    1. Fisher information

    The Fisher information 5.7 for the whole sample is thus \mathcal{I}_n(\lambda) = -\mathrm{E}\left[-\frac{n}{\lambda^2}\right] = \frac{n}{\lambda^2}.

    Evaluating the expected Fisher information at the MLE gives \mathcal{I}_n(\hat{\lambda}) = \frac{n}{\hat{\lambda}^2} = n\bar{x}^2. Here the observed information is j_n(\lambda;\underline x)=n/\lambda^2, so it agrees with expected information for every \lambda.

    1. Asymptotic posterior distribution

    By Theorem 6.2 \lambda \mid \underline{x} \overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\!\left( \hat{\lambda},\; \mathcal{I}_n(\hat{\lambda})^{-1} \right) = \mathcal{N}\!\left( \frac{1}{\bar{x}},\; \frac{1}{n\bar{x}^{2}} \right).

  2. Plugging in data

    For n = 200 and \bar{x} = 7.14, \hat{\lambda} = \frac{1}{ 7.14} \approx 0.14, and the variance is \frac{1}{n\bar{x}^2} = \frac{1}{200 \cdot 7.14^2} \approx 9.807 \times 10^{-5}.

    Thus the asymptotic posterior is \lambda \mid \underline{x} \approx \mathcal{N}\!\left(0.14,\; 9.807 \times 10^{-5}\right).

The p-value is approximately \mathrm{Pr}(T_{39} \le -1.73) \approx 0.046, which is greater than 0.01.

Conclusion (t-test): There is insufficient evidence at the 1% significance level to conclude that the product’s mean sodium content is below the regulatory limit.

Both tests lead to the same conclusion. The t-test is slightly more conservative due to the additional uncertainty in estimating the variance.

Important 44: MAP Estimation in a Defect-Rate Problem

A factory produces electronic components, each of which may be defective with some unknown probability p.
To assess the defect rate, engineers inspect a random sample of n = 40 components and find that
x = 7
are defective.

To incorporate previous experience, the quality-control team places a prior distribution
p \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a,b), \qquad a = 2,\; b = 8.

You may assume the data follow the model X \mid p \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n,p).

  1. Derive the posterior distribution \pi(p \mid x).
  2. Compute the Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) estimator \hat{p}_{\mathrm{MAP}}.
  3. Interpret your MAP estimate in the context of the defect-rate problem.
Solution

  1. Posterior Distribution

    The likelihood is f(x \mid p) = \binom{n}{x} p^{\,x} (1-p)^{\,n-x}.

    The prior is \pi(p) = \frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)} p^{\,a-1}(1-p)^{\,b-1}.

    Therefore the posterior (dropping constants not depending on p) is \pi(p \mid x) \propto p^{\,x+a-1} (1-p)^{\,n-x+b-1}.

    This is recognised as a Beta distribution: p \mid x \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a + x,\; b + n - x) = \mathrm{Beta}(2 + 7,\; 8 + 40 - 7) = \mathrm{Beta}(9,\; 41).

  2. MAP Estimator

    For a \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta) distribution with \alpha>1 and \beta>1, the mode is provided in closed form, yielding the MAP: \hat{p}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2}.

    Here \alpha = 9 and \beta = 41, so \hat{p}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \frac{9 - 1}{9 + 41 - 2} = \frac{8}{48} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.1667.

    Alternatively, we can derive this using the approach outlined here 6.4:

    We have \pi(p \mid x) \propto p^{\,x+a-1} (1-p)^{\,n-x+b-1}. Taking logs: \log \pi(p \mid x) = (x + a - 1)\log p + (n - x + b - 1)\log(1 - p) + C, for some p-independent constant C.

    Differentiating with respect to p: \frac{\partial}{\partial p} \log \pi(p \mid x) = \frac{x + a - 1}{p} - \frac{n - x + b - 1}{1 - p}.

    Setting this equal to 0 and solving for p: \begin{align*} \frac{x + a - 1}{p} - \frac{n - x + b - 1}{1 - p} &= 0 \\[4pt] \frac{x + a - 1}{p} &= \frac{n - x + b - 1}{1 - p} \\[4pt] (x + a - 1)(1 - p) &= (n - x + b - 1)p \\[4pt] x + a - 1 &= (n - x + b - 1)p + (x + a - 1)p \\[4pt] x + a - 1 &= (n + a + b - 2)p \\[4pt] \Rightarrow\quad \hat{p}_{\text{MAP}} &= \frac{x + a - 1}{n + a + b - 2}. \end{align*}

    Plugging in n = 40,\; x = 7,\; a = 2,\; b = 8 gives \hat{p}_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{7 + 2 - 1}{40 + 2 + 8 - 2} = \frac{8}{48} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.1667.

  3. Interpretation

    Based on both prior information and the observed data, the MAP estimator suggests that the defect probability is approximately \hat{p}_{\mathrm{MAP}} \approx 0.17.

    This is slightly lower than the raw sample proportion
    \frac{x}{n} = \frac{7}{40} = 0.175, reflecting the influence of the prior belief that defects are relatively uncommon.

Important 45: MAP Estimation for a Call-Arrival Rate

A customer support centre receives phone calls according to a Poisson process with unknown rate \lambda (calls per hour).

To model the long-run arrival rate, analysts place a prior distribution \lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a,b), \qquad a = 3,\; b = 1.5, where we use the shape–rate parameterisation: \lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a,b) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \pi(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda}.

Over a monitoring period of T = 10 hours, they observe a total of y = 47 calls.

Assume that, conditional on \lambda, the total number of calls in T hours follows Y \mid \lambda \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(T\lambda).

  1. Derive the posterior distribution \pi(\lambda \mid y).
  2. Compute the Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) estimator \hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{MAP}}.
  3. Interpret your MAP estimate in the context of the call-arrival rate (give its units and practical meaning).
Solution

  1. Posterior Distribution

    The likelihood for the total number of calls Y in T hours, given \lambda, is f(y \mid \lambda) = \Pr(Y = y \mid \lambda) = \frac{(T\lambda)^y e^{-T\lambda}}{y!}.

    The prior is \pi(\lambda) = \frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)} \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda}, \qquad a = 3,\; b = 1.5, using the shape–rate parameterisation.

    Therefore, the posterior density (dropping constants not depending on \lambda) is \pi(\lambda \mid y) \propto \lambda^{\,y} e^{-T\lambda} \cdot \lambda^{\,a-1} e^{-b\lambda} = \lambda^{\,y+a-1} e^{-(T+b)\lambda}.

    This is recognised as a Gamma distribution with updated shape and rate: \lambda \mid y \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(a + y,\; b + T) = \mathrm{Gamma}(3 + 47,\; 1.5 + 10) = \mathrm{Gamma}(50,\; 11.5).

  2. MAP Estimator

    For a \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta) distribution (shape–rate) with \alpha > 1, the mode is \lambda_{\text{mode}} = \frac{\alpha - 1}{\beta}, which is also the MAP estimator in this conjugate setting: \hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \frac{\alpha - 1}{\beta}.

    Here \alpha = 50 and \beta = 11.5, so \hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \frac{50 - 1}{11.5} = \frac{49}{11.5} \approx 4.26 \;\text{calls per hour}.

    Alternatively, we can derive this by maximising the log-posterior directly (as in the general MAP procedure 6.4):

    From above, \pi(\lambda \mid y) \propto \lambda^{\,y+a-1} e^{-(T+b)\lambda}. Taking logs, \log \pi(\lambda \mid y) = (y + a - 1)\log \lambda - (T + b)\lambda + C, for some \lambda-independent constant C.

    Differentiating with respect to \lambda: \frac{\partial}{\partial \lambda} \log \pi(\lambda \mid y) = \frac{y + a - 1}{\lambda} - (T + b).

    Setting this equal to 0 and solving: \begin{align*} \frac{y + a - 1}{\lambda} - (T + b) &= 0 \\[4pt] \frac{y + a - 1}{\lambda} &= T + b \\[4pt] \lambda &= \frac{y + a - 1}{T + b}. \end{align*}

    Substituting y = 47,\; a = 3,\; T = 10,\; b = 1.5 gives \hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \frac{47 + 3 - 1}{10 + 1.5} = \frac{49}{11.5} \approx 4.26, consistent with the mode formula above.

  3. Interpretation

    The MAP estimate suggests that the underlying call-arrival rate is approximately \hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{MAP}} \approx 4.26 \;\text{calls per hour}.

    For comparison, the MLE based only on the data is \hat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{MLE}} = \frac{y}{T} = \frac{47}{10} = 4.7 \;\text{calls per hour}.

    The MAP estimate is slightly lower than the MLE, reflecting the influence of the prior belief
    (encoded by the \mathrm{Gamma}(3,1.5) prior) that the call rate is somewhat smaller than what the current data alone would suggest.

Important 46: Unbiased Estimation and Efficiency in the Normal Model

Suppose that X_1, \ldots, X_n are independent \mathcal{N}(\mu, \theta) random variables, where \mu is a known constant and \theta is the unknown parameter of interest. Let \bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i.

  1. Let \hat{\theta} = \frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar{X})^2 be an estimator of \theta.

    1. Determine the distribution of \bar{X} and hence show that
      \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}] = \mu and \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}^2] = \theta/n + \mu^2.

    2. Show that \mathrm{E}[X_i^2] = \theta + \mu^2 for any i = 1, \ldots, n.

    3. Using the fact that \mathrm{E}[X_i\bar{X}] = \theta/n + \mu^2 for any i, or by another method,
      derive the value of c that ensures \hat{\theta} is unbiased.

  2. Show that for any unbiased estimator \hat{\theta} of \theta, it holds that \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}] \geq \frac{2\theta^2}{n}.

  3. Let \hat{\theta}' = \frac{1}{n+2}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)^2 be another estimator of \theta.

    Determine whether \hat{\theta}' has a lower mean-squared error (MSE) than any unbiased estimator of \theta.

    Hint: If X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \theta), then \mathrm{Var}[(X-\mu)^2] = 2\theta^2.

Solution

(1a)
Since \bar{X} is the sample mean of i.i.d. normals, it follows that
\bar{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \theta/n).
Hence: \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}] = \mu, \qquad \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}^2] = \mathrm{Var}[\bar{X}] + \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}]^2 = \frac{\theta}{n} + \mu^2.

(1b)
For any i, \mathrm{E}[X_i^2] = \mathrm{Var}[X_i] + \mathrm{E}[X_i]^2 = \theta + \mu^2.

(1c)
We have: \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] = \frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[(X_i - \bar{X})^2] = \frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=1}^n \Big(\mathrm{E}[X_i^2] - 2\mathrm{E}[X_i\bar{X}] + \mathrm{E}[\bar{X}^2]\Big).

Substituting known expectations: \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] = \frac{1}{c}\sum_{i=1}^n \big(\theta+\mu^2 - 2(\theta/n+\mu^2) + \theta/n + \mu^2\big) = \frac{n-1}{c}\theta.

Thus, \hat{\theta} is unbiased if and only if c = n-1.

(2)

The likelihood is: L(\theta) = (2\pi\theta)^{-n/2} \exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\mu)^2\right].

Hence the log-likelihood: \ell(\theta) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\log(\theta) - \frac{1}{2\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\mu)^2.

Differentiating twice: \frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \theta^2} = \frac{n}{2\theta^2} - \frac{1}{\theta^3}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2.

The Fisher information is: \mathcal{I}(\theta) = -\mathrm{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \theta^2}\right] = \frac{n}{2\theta^2}.

By the Cramér–Rao inequality: \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}] \ge \frac{1}{\mathcal{I}(\theta)} = \frac{2\theta^2}{n}.

(3)

Compute the bias: \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}'] = \frac{1}{n+2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[(X_i - \mu)^2] = \frac{n\theta}{n+2}, \qquad \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}') = -\frac{2\theta}{n+2}.

Variance: \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}'] = \frac{1}{(n+2)^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}[(X_i - \mu)^2] = \frac{2n\theta^2}{(n+2)^2}.

Hence: \mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\theta}') = \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}'] + \mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}')^2 = \frac{2n\theta^2}{(n+2)^2} + \frac{4\theta^2}{(n+2)^2} = \frac{2\theta^2}{n+2}.

Since \frac{2\theta^2}{n+2} < \frac{2\theta^2}{n} for all n > 0,
\hat{\theta}' has a smaller mean-squared error than any unbiased estimator of \theta.

Important 49: Properties of the Maximum Likelihood Estimator

    \hat{\theta} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \log(1 + x_i).

  3. Using the fact that \mathrm{E}[\log(1 + X)] = \theta, show that \hat{\theta} is an unbiased estimator for \theta.

  4. Is \hat{\theta} also an asymptotically unbiased estimator for \theta?

Solution

(a) Derivation of the log-likelihood

The likelihood function is
L(\theta; \underline{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \theta) = \theta^{-n} \prod_{i=1}^n (1 + x_i)^{-\frac{1+\theta}{\theta}}. Taking logs gives
\ell(\theta \mid \underline{x}) = -n \log \theta - \frac{1+\theta}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n \log(1 + x_i).

\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} = -\frac{n}{\theta} + \frac{1}{\theta^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \log(1 + x_i). Setting this equal to zero:
-\frac{n}{\hat{\theta}} + \frac{1}{\hat{\theta}^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \log(1 + x_i) = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \hat{\theta} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \log(1 + x_i).

\mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{E}[\log(1 + X_i)] = \frac{1}{n} \cdot n\theta = \theta. Hence, \hat{\theta} is unbiased.

(d) Asymptotic unbiasedness

Since \hat{\theta} is unbiased for every finite n,
\lim_{n \to \infty} \mathrm{E}[\hat{\theta}] = \theta. Therefore, \hat{\theta} is also asymptotically unbiased.

(2)

\begin{align*} P(\hat{\theta} \le x) &= 1 - P(\hat{\theta} > x) = 1 - P(X_1 > x, \ldots, X_n > x) \\ &= 1 - (1 - F_X(x))^n. \end{align*}

(3)

Thus, f_Z(z) = f_{\hat{\theta}}(z) for all z \in \mathbb{R}, so \hat{\theta} has the same distribution as Z.

Hence, \mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = (1 - \theta)\frac{1}{n + 1}.

Variance: \begin{align*} \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}] &= \mathrm{Var}[Z] = (1 - \theta)^2 \mathrm{Var}[Y] = (1 - \theta)^2 \frac{n}{(n + 1)^2 (n + 2)}. \end{align*}

As n \to \infty, both the bias and variance tend to 0: \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = 0, \qquad \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathrm{Var}[\hat{\theta}] = 0.

Therefore, \hat{\theta} is a consistent estimator of \theta.

Important 56: Conjugacy: Normal Model with known mean, Inverse Gamma Prior for variance

Suppose that we observe n i.i.d. Normal Normal observations X_i \mid \gamma \; \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \; \mathcal{N}(\mu, \gamma), where the observation mean \mu is a known quantity. Show that choosing a Inverse Gamma Inverse Gamma prior \gamma \sim \operatorname{InvGamma}(a,b) yields the posterior distribution \gamma \mid \underline{x} \sim \mathrm{InvGamma}\left(a + n/2,\; b + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 \right).

Solution

We compute the posterior using Bayes theorem: \pi(\gamma \mid \underline{x}) \propto \pi(\gamma) f(\underline{x}\mid \gamma).

  1. Prior PDF: Since \gamma \sim \operatorname{InvGamma}(a,b), its probability density function is \pi(\gamma) = \frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)} \gamma^{-(a+1)} \exp\left(-\frac{b}{\gamma}\right), \qquad \gamma>0. Ignoring the factors which do not depend on \gamma, this is proportional to \pi(\gamma) \propto \gamma^{-(a+1)} \exp\left\{-\frac{b}{\gamma}\right\}.

  2. Likelihood: Since we have n i.i.d. observations X_i \mid \gamma \; \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \; \mathcal{N}(\mu, \gamma), the likelihood is \begin{align*} f(\underline{x} \mid \gamma) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\gamma}} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\gamma}(x_i - \mu)^2\right\} \\ &= (2\pi\gamma)^{-n/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2\right\}. \end{align*} Dropping the factors that do not depend on \gamma, this is proportional to f(\underline{x} \mid \gamma) \propto \gamma^{-n/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2\right\}.

  3. Posterior: Using the prior and likelihood derived above, we have \begin{align*} \pi(\gamma \mid \underline{x}) &\propto \gamma^{-(a+1)} \exp\left\{-\frac{b}{\gamma}\right\} \; \gamma^{-n/2} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2\right\} \\ &\propto \gamma^{-(a + n/2 + 1)} \exp\left\{ -\frac{1}{\gamma}\left( b + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 \right) \right\}. \end{align*} This takes the form of an Inverse Gamma density function and thus \gamma \mid \underline{x} \sim \mathrm{InvGamma}\left(a + \frac{n}{2},\; b + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 \right), so the Inverse Gamma distribution is conjugate 6.3.

Important 57: Conjugacy: Exponential Model, Gamma Prior

Suppose that we observe n i.i.d. Exponential Exponential observations X_i \mid \lambda \; \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \; \mathrm{Exponential}(\lambda). Show that choosing a Gamma Gamma prior \lambda \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(a,b) yields the posterior distribution \lambda \mid \underline{x} \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\left(a + n,\; b + n\bar{x} \right).

Solution

We compute the posterior using Bayes theorem: \pi(\lambda \mid \underline{x}) \propto \pi(\lambda) \, f(\underline{x}\mid \lambda).

  1. Prior PDF: Since \lambda \sim \operatorname{Gamma}(a,b), its probability density function is \pi(\lambda) = \frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)} \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda} Ignoring the factors which do not depend on \lambda, this is proportional to \pi(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda}.

  2. Likelihood: Since we have n i.i.d. observations X_i \mid \lambda \; \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \; \mathrm{Exponential}(\lambda), the likelihood is \begin{align*} f(\underline{x} \mid \lambda) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda \, e^{-\lambda x_i} \\ &= \lambda^n \, \exp\left\{-\lambda \, n\bar{x}\right\} \end{align*} Note that there are no factors that do not depend on \lambda.

  3. Posterior: Using the prior and likelihood derived above, we have \begin{align*} \pi(\lambda \mid \underline{x}) &\propto \lambda^{a-1} e^{-b\lambda} \; \lambda^n \, \exp\left\{-\lambda \, n\bar{x}\right\} \\ &\propto \lambda^{a + n - 1} \exp\left\{ -\lambda(b + n\bar{x}) \right\} \end{align*} This takes the form of a Gamma density function and thus \lambda \mid \underline{x} \sim \mathrm{Gamma}\left(a + n,\; b + n\bar{x} \right), so the Gamma distribution is conjugate 6.3.

Important 58: Conjugacy: Weibull Model with known rate, Inverse Gamma Prior

Suppose that we observe n i.i.d. Weibull Weibull observations X_i \mid \theta \; \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \; \operatorname{Weibull}(\beta,\theta), where the parameter \beta is a known quantity. Show that choosing a Inverse Gamma Inverse Gamma prior \theta \sim \operatorname{InvGamma}(a,b) yields the posterior distribution \theta \mid \underline{x} \sim \mathrm{InvGamma}\left(a + n,\; b + \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^\beta \right).

Solution

We compute the posterior using Bayes theorem: \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) \propto \pi(\theta) \, f(\underline{x}\mid \theta).

  1. Prior PDF: Since \theta \sim \operatorname{InvGamma}(a,b), its probability density function is \pi(\theta) = \frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)} \theta^{-(a+1)} e^{-b\theta^{-1}}. Ignoring the factors which do not depend on \theta, this is proportional to \pi(\theta) \propto \theta^{-(a+1)} \exp\left\{-\frac{b}{\theta}\right\}.

  2. Likelihood: Since we have n i.i.d. observations X_i \mid \theta \; \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \; \mathrm{Weibull}(\beta, \theta), the likelihood is \begin{align*} f(\underline{x} \mid \theta) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{\beta}{\theta}x_i^{\beta - 1} \exp\left(-\frac{x_i^\beta}{\theta}\right) \\ &= \frac{\beta^n}{\theta^n} \exp\left\{- \frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^\beta\right\} \left(\prod_{i=1}^n x_i \right)^{\beta - 1} \end{align*} Ignoring the factors which do not depend on \theta, this is proportional to f(\underline{x} \mid \theta) \propto \theta^{-n} \exp\left\{- \frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^\beta\right\} .

  3. Posterior: Using the prior and likelihood derived above, we have \begin{align*} \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) &\propto \theta^{-(a+1)} \exp\left\{-\frac{b}{\theta}\right\} \; \theta^{-n} \exp\left\{- \frac{1}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^\beta\right\}\\ &\propto \theta^{-(a + n + 1)} \exp\left\{-\frac{1}{\theta}\left(b + \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^\beta \right)\right\}. \end{align*} This takes the form of an Inverse Gamma density function and thus \theta \mid \underline{x} \sim \mathrm{InvGamma}\left(a + n,\; b + \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^\beta \right), so the Inverse Gamma distribution is conjugate 6.3.

Important 59: Posterior calculation: Normal likelihood, Normal prior

Consider the setup of Conjugacy: Normal Model, Normal Prior 55.
Suppose the observation variance is known and equal to \sigma^2 = 4, and the prior for the mean is \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2), \qquad \mu_0 = 0, \ \sigma_0^2 = 1.

We observe n = 10 i.i.d. observations with sample mean \bar{x} = 1.5.

Using the closed-form update M = \frac{\frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{n\bar{x}}{\sigma^2}}{ \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}}, \qquad V = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}}, compute the posterior distribution \mu \mid \underline{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(M, V).

Solution

The prior parameters are \mu_0 = 0 and \sigma^2_0 = 1, the data terms are \sigma^2 = 4, n=10 and \bar{x} = 1.5.

Therefore, we have \begin{align*} M &= \frac{\frac{0}{1} + \frac{10\times 1.5}{4}}{ \frac{1}{1} + \frac{10}{4}} = \frac{15}{14} \\ V &= \frac{1}{ \frac{1}{1} + \frac{10}{4}} = \frac{4}{14}. \end{align*}

Important 60: Model, parameter, estimator, and estimate

Waiting times X_1,\ldots,X_4 are modelled as i.i.d. Exponential random variables with unknown rate \lambda>0, so f(x\mid\lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x},\qquad x>0, \qquad \mathrm{E}[X_i]=1/\lambda. The observed waiting times, in minutes, are 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 3.

  1. Identify the parameter and its parameter space. What does i.i.d. assume?
  2. Calculate \bar x and the observed value of the rate estimator \hat\lambda=4/\sum_i X_i. State the units of each.
  3. Which of \bar X, 4/\sum_i X_i, and \bar X-1/\lambda are statistics? Explain the distinction between an estimator and an estimate.
Solution

(a) The parameter is the rate \lambda, with parameter space (0,\infty). The i.i.d. assumption says the waiting times are mutually independent and all have the same Exponential distribution, including the same rate.

(b) The observed total is 6 minutes, giving \bar x=\frac64=1.5\text{ minutes},\qquad \hat\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac46=\frac23\text{ per minute}. The mean waiting time and the rate are different targets, with reciprocal units.

(c) Both \bar X and 4/\sum_i X_i are statistics: their values can be computed from the observations without knowing the parameter. The quantity \bar X-1/\lambda is not a statistic because it depends on the unknown rate. An estimator is a statistic used to estimate a target; it is random before sampling. Its realised numerical value, such as 2/3 per minute, is an estimate.

Important 61: Unbiased and consistent are different properties

Let X_1,\ldots,X_n be i.i.d. \mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2), where \sigma^2>0. Consider two estimators of \mu: T_n=X_1,\qquad U_n=\bar X+\frac1n.

  1. Find the bias and variance of each estimator.
  2. Show directly that T_n is not consistent by considering \mathrm{Pr}(|T_n-\mu|>\varepsilon) for a fixed \varepsilon>0.
  3. Use the sufficient condition based on bias and variance to show that U_n is consistent. What do these examples show about unbiasedness?
Solution

(a) For every n, \mathrm{Bias}(T_n)=0,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(T_n)=\sigma^2, whereas \mathrm{Bias}(U_n)=\frac1n,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(U_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}. Adding a constant changes the mean but not the variance.

(b) The distribution of T_n does not change with n: \mathrm{Pr}(|T_n-\mu|>\varepsilon) =2\left[1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma}\right)\right]>0. This fixed positive probability cannot tend to zero, so T_n is not consistent. It ignores every observation after the first. Merely failing a sufficient condition would not prove inconsistency; this calculation does.

(c) Both the bias 1/n and variance \sigma^2/n tend to zero, so U_n is consistent. Equivalently, \mathrm{MSE}(U_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\frac1{n^2}\longrightarrow0. Thus an unbiased estimator need not be consistent, and an estimator can be biased at every finite sample size yet still be consistent. Unbiasedness concerns the mean at a given sample size; consistency concerns concentration around the target as the sample size grows.

Important 62: Likelihood is not a posterior probability

Four conditionally independent Bernoulli trials produce the ordered outcomes (1,1,1,0). The common success probability \theta can take only the values 1/4 and 3/4.

  1. Calculate the likelihood at each possible parameter value and their ratio.
  2. A student says: “The larger likelihood is the probability that \theta=3/4.” Explain the error.
  3. Now assign prior probabilities \mathrm{Pr}(\theta=1/4)=0.8 and \mathrm{Pr}(\theta=3/4)=0.2. Find the posterior probability of \theta=3/4.
  4. If only the total number of successes had been recorded, how would the likelihoods and the answer to part (c) change?
Solution

(a) For this ordered sequence the likelihood is L(\theta)=\theta^3(1-\theta): L(1/4)=\frac3{256},\qquad L(3/4)=\frac{27}{256},\qquad \frac{L(3/4)}{L(1/4)}=9. The observed sequence is nine times as probable under \theta=3/4 as under \theta=1/4.

(b) Each likelihood is the probability of the observed data given a parameter value. It is not the probability of that parameter given the data. Likelihood values need not sum to one over parameter values. A Bayesian posterior requires a prior and normalisation.

(c) Multiply by the prior weights and normalise: \mathrm{Pr}(\theta=3/4\mid\underline x) =\frac{(27/256)(0.2)}{(3/256)(0.8)+(27/256)(0.2)} =\frac9{13}\approx0.6923. Normalising the likelihoods alone would give 0.9, which would correspond to equal prior probabilities, not the prior specified here.

(d) The count has a \mathrm{Binomial}(4,\theta) distribution. Both likelihoods acquire the same factor \binom43=4, becoming 3/64 and 27/64. That factor cancels from the ratio and from the posterior, so part (c) is unchanged. The probability of a count differs from that of one ordered sequence.

Important 63: Choosing a method from the assumptions

In each scenario below, the observations form an i.i.d. sample and the target is the population mean \mu. For a confidence interval or a test of H_0:\mu=\mu_0, choose an appropriate method and state whether its calibration is exact or approximate. Give the statistic and reference distribution when available.

  1. n=12 observations from a Normal population with known variance \sigma^2.
  2. n=12 observations from a Normal population with unknown variance.
  3. n=200 observations from a non-Normal population with finite, nonzero variance.
  4. n=8 observations from a strongly skewed population with unknown variance. Is an exact Normal-theory t procedure justified by the information given?
Solution

(a) Use an exact Normal z procedure. Under H_0, Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim\mathcal N(0,1). Normality of the observations makes this exact even for n=12.

(b) Use an exact one-sample t procedure. With the usual sample standard deviation S, under H_0, T=\frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{11}. The degrees of freedom are n-1, and replacing \sigma by S introduces uncertainty that the t distribution accounts for.

(c) A CLT-based Normal procedure is a possible large-sample approximation: \frac{\bar X-\mu_0}{S/\sqrt n}\overset{\mathrm{approx.}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,1) \quad\text{under }H_0. Its finite-sample accuracy depends on the population’s shape, including its tails. A sample size of 200 does not guarantee a good approximation for every finite-variance distribution.

(d) No. An exact t distribution relies on the Normal sampling model, and n=8 with strong skewness gives little support for a CLT approximation. More information about the population model or a different justified method is needed. Unknown variance alone does not make a t procedure exact.

Important 64: What a 95% interval does and does not say

An i.i.d. Normal sample has known population standard deviation \sigma=8, sample size n=64, and observed mean \bar x=15. Use z_{0.975}=1.96.

  1. Calculate a 95\% confidence interval for \mu.
  2. Explain what is correct or incorrect about each statement:
  1. “This method produces intervals containing the true mean in 95\% of repeated samples under the model.”
  2. “There is now a 95\% frequentist probability that \mu lies between these two observed endpoints.”
  3. “About 95\% of individual observations lie in this interval.”
  1. How would the interval’s width change if the sample size increased to 256, with the same known population standard deviation and confidence level?
  2. If a Bayesian analysis instead reported a 95\% credible interval, what probability statement would that support?
Solution

(a) The standard error is 8/\sqrt{64}=1, so the interval is 15\pm1.96(1)=[13.04,16.96].

(b) Statement 1 is the correct repeated-sampling interpretation (up to rounding of the Normal quantile). Before sampling, the endpoints are random. Statement 2 incorrectly assigns frequentist probability to a fixed parameter lying in a now fixed interval; the realised interval either contains \mu or it does not. Statement 3 confuses uncertainty about a mean with the spread of observations. Individual observations have standard deviation 8, whereas the sample mean has standard error 1.

(c) Width is proportional to 1/\sqrt n. Increasing n by a factor of four halves the width from 3.92 to 1.96. It need not leave the centre unchanged, because a new sample may have a different mean.

(d) For a credible interval [l,u] one can write \mathrm{Pr}(l\le\mu\le u\mid\underline x)=0.95, with probability taken under the posterior distribution, conditional on the data and the chosen prior and sampling model. A frequentist confidence interval cannot be given that interpretation without a Bayesian analysis.

Important 65: The alternative determines the p-value

A test statistic for a Normal mean has an exact standard Normal distribution under H_0:\mu=\mu_0. Its observed value is z_{\mathrm{obs}}=1.80. You may use \Phi(1.80)=0.9641.

  1. Calculate the p-value for the pre-specified alternative H_1:\mu>\mu_0.
  2. Calculate the p-value for the pre-specified alternative H_1:\mu\ne\mu_0.
  3. State the decision at the 5\% level in each case. Does either p-value give the probability that H_0 is true?
  4. Explain why choosing a one-sided alternative after seeing the sign of the observed statistic is problematic.
Solution

(a) For the right-sided alternative, larger values are more extreme: p=\mathrm{Pr}_{H_0}(Z\ge1.80)=1-0.9641=0.0359.

(b) For the two-sided alternative, both tails count: p=\mathrm{Pr}_{H_0}(|Z|\ge1.80)=2(1-0.9641)=0.0718.

(c) Reject H_0 for the right-sided test since 0.0359<0.05. Fail to reject it for the two-sided test since 0.0718>0.05. This latter decision does not prove \mu=\mu_0. Each p-value is a tail probability for a statistic assuming the null model, not a posterior probability of that model.

(d) The rejection rule must be specified before inspecting the outcome. If we always choose the observed direction and use a 5\% one-sided cutoff, we reject in either tail beyond \pm z_{0.95}. Under the continuous standard Normal null this has probability 0.05+0.05=0.10, not 0.05.

Important 66: Type I error, Type II error, and power

Let X_1,\ldots,X_{16} be i.i.d. \mathcal N(\mu,64) observations. Test H_0:\mu=20 against H_1:\mu>20 by rejecting when Z=\frac{\bar X-20}{8/\sqrt{16}}>1.645. For these calculations use \Phi(1.645)\approx0.95, \Phi(0.355)\approx0.6387, and \Phi(2.355)\approx0.9907.

  1. Express the rejection region in terms of \bar X and calculate the Type I error probability.
  2. If the true mean is \mu=24, find the power and Type II error probability.
  3. Repeat the power calculation for n=64, using the same 5\% one-sided test. Explain why the Type I error probability stays the same while power changes.
Solution

(a) The standard error is 2, so reject when \bar X>20+1.645(2)=23.29. Under H_0, Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1), giving \mathrm{Pr}_{\mu=20}(\text{reject }H_0)=1-\Phi(1.645)\approx0.05. A Type I error is rejecting when the null is true.

(b) Under \mu=24, \bar X\sim\mathcal N(24,4), so \mathrm{Power}(24)=\mathrm{Pr}_{\mu=24}(\bar X>23.29) =1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{23.29-24}{2}\right) =\Phi(0.355)\approx0.6387. The Type II error probability at \mu=24 is 1-0.6387=0.3613: failing to reject when this alternative value is true. Power is a function of the true mean, not a single probability shared by all alternatives.

(c) With n=64 the standard error is 1, so the rejection boundary becomes 20+1.645=21.645. At \mu=24, \mathrm{Power}(24)=1-\Phi(21.645-24)=\Phi(2.355)\approx0.9907. The null-standardised statistic is still standard Normal and uses the same cutoff, maintaining size approximately 0.05 (the approximation is only from rounding the cutoff). The smaller standard error makes a mean of 24 easier to distinguish from 20.

Important 67: Sequential Bayesian updating and prior influence

Conditional on a fixed success probability p, all trials below are independent Bernoulli trials. Begin with the prior p\sim\mathrm{Beta}(2,3). A first batch contains 3 successes in 5 trials; a second batch contains 1 success in 3 trials.

  1. Find the posterior after each batch, using the first posterior as the prior for the second update.
  2. Check that updating once with all the data gives the same final posterior. Find its posterior mean and MAP estimate.
  3. Express the final posterior mean as a weighted average of the original prior mean and the overall observed success proportion.
  4. Repeat the final posterior mean calculation for a \mathrm{Beta}(20,30) prior. Explain the effect of this change.

You may use that a \mathrm{Beta}(a,b) distribution has mean a/(a+b) and, when a,b>1, mode (a-1)/(a+b-2).

Solution

(a) A Beta update adds successes to the first shape parameter and failures to the second. The first batch has 2 failures, giving p\mid\text{batch 1}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(2+3,3+2)=\mathrm{Beta}(5,5). The second batch has 2 failures, so p\mid\text{both batches}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(5+1,5+2)=\mathrm{Beta}(6,7).

(b) There are 4 successes and 4 failures overall. One update gives \mathrm{Beta}(2+4,3+4)=\mathrm{Beta}(6,7), as above. Conditional independence lets us multiply the batch likelihoods in either order. The posterior summaries are \mathrm{E}[p\mid\underline x]=\frac6{13}\approx0.4615,\qquad \hat p_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\frac5{11}\approx0.4545. The posterior mean and mode need not coincide.

(c) The original prior mean is 2/5=0.4 and the sample proportion is 4/8=0.5. Thus \frac6{13}=\frac5{13}(0.4)+\frac8{13}(0.5). The weights are proportional to the prior shape sum a+b and the number of trials. The result lies between the prior mean and the observed proportion.

(d) The new prior has the same mean 0.4 but is more concentrated. The posterior is \mathrm{Beta}(24,34), with mean \frac{24}{58}=\frac{50}{58}(0.4)+\frac8{58}(0.5) =\frac{12}{29}\approx0.4138. The larger prior weight keeps the posterior mean closer to 0.4 for these eight trials. This illustrates how prior concentration affects an update even when the prior mean is unchanged.

Important 68: Equal-tailed and highest-density credible intervals

Suppose the posterior density of a parameter \theta is \pi(\theta\mid\underline x)=2\theta,\qquad 0\le\theta\le1, and zero elsewhere.

  1. Find the posterior CDF and its quantile of order p, for 0<p<1.
  2. Find a 90\% equal-tailed credible interval.
  3. Find a 90\% highest-density interval (HDI). Explain why its upper endpoint is 1.
  4. Compare the intervals’ lengths and state what they have in common.
Solution

(a) On the support, F(\theta\mid\underline x)=\int_0^\theta2t\,\mathrm{d}t=\theta^2. The CDF is zero below 0 and one above 1. Solving q_p^2=p gives q_p=\sqrt p.

(b) Leave 0.05 posterior probability in each tail: [q_{0.05},q_{0.95}]=[\sqrt{0.05},\sqrt{0.95}] \approx[0.2236,0.9747].

(c) The density increases throughout the support. The highest-density values therefore form an interval [a,1]. Requiring posterior mass 0.90 gives \int_a^1 2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta=1-a^2=0.90, so the HDI is [\sqrt{0.10},1]\approx[0.3162,1]. Every interior point of this interval has higher density than points excluded below it. There is no requirement for equal tail probabilities, or equal endpoint densities when an endpoint is a boundary of the support.

(d) The equal-tailed interval has length approximately 0.7511; the HDI has length approximately 0.6838. Both contain 90\% of the posterior probability, but the HDI is shorter here because it uses the part of the support with greatest density. Neither says that future observations fall in the interval with probability 0.90: these are intervals for the parameter.
