10  Bayesian Hypothesis Testing (not examinable)

Recalling the main inference tasks 3.2, we now move from interval estimation to hypothesis testing in the Bayesian framework, and consider the Bayesian analogue of frequentist hypothesis testing.

NoteLearning objectives

By the end of this optional chapter, you should be able to:

  • explain why a point null needs positive prior probability;
  • compute posterior hypothesis odds from a Bayes factor and prior odds; and
  • distinguish a Bayes factor, a posterior hypothesis probability and a frequentist p-value.

The Bayesian perspective is different from the frequentist one: instead of calibrating a rejection rule under repeated samples from H_0, we compare how well the competing hypotheses predicted the observed data.

How probable is each hypothesis given the observed data?

10.1 The Bayesian Setup

Just like in frequentist hypothesis testing 9, the Bayesian approach compares two hypotheses:

H_0 : \theta \in \Theta_0, \qquad H_1 : \theta \in \Theta_1, where \Theta_1 = \Theta \setminus \Theta_0.

The Bayesian perspective is to assign probabilities to hypotheses and to parameter values.
This involves two ingredients:

  1. Prior probabilities of the hypotheses: \Pr(H_0) \quad \text{and} \quad \Pr(H_1),

    such that \Pr(H_0) + \Pr(H_1) = 1.

  2. Conditional priors for the parameter under each hypothesis: \pi(\theta \mid H_0) \quad \text{and} \quad \pi(\theta \mid H_1).

Equivalently, introduce a hypothesis indicator H\in\{0,1\}, assign \Pr(H=j)>0, and then specify the distribution of \theta conditional on H=j. This mixture construction is essential for a point null: a continuous prior on \theta alone gives \Pr(\theta=\theta_0)=0. For H_0:\theta=\theta_0, the conditional prior under H_0 is a point mass at \theta_0.

10.1.1 Posterior Probabilities of Hypotheses

After observing data \underline{x}, Bayes’ theorem gives \Pr(H_0 \mid \underline{x}) = \frac{f(\underline{x}\mid H_0)\,\Pr(H_0)}{f(\underline{x})}.

If H_0 is not simple, the likelihood f(\underline{x}\mid H_0) is obtained by averaging over \theta \in \Theta_0 with the conditional prior: f(\underline{x} \mid H_0) = \int_{\Theta_0} f(\underline{x}\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta \mid H_0)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

For the simple null H_0:\theta=\theta_0, this reduces to m_0(\underline{x})=f(\underline{x}\mid\theta_0).

So we can write \Pr(H_0 \mid \underline{x}) = \frac{m_0(\underline{x}) \,\Pr(H_0)}{m(\underline{x})},

where

  • m_0(\underline{x}) = \int_{\Theta_0} f(\underline{x}\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta\mid H_0)\,\mathrm{d}\theta is the marginal likelihood under H_0,
  • m_1(\underline{x}) = \int_{\Theta_1} f(\underline{x}\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta\mid H_1)\,\mathrm{d}\theta is the marginal likelihood under H_1,
  • m(\underline{x}) = m_0(\underline{x})\Pr(H_0) + m_1(\underline{x})\Pr(H_1) is the overall marginal likelihood.

Thus the Bayesian procedure directly yields the posterior probabilities \Pr(H_0 \mid \underline{x}) and \Pr(H_1 \mid \underline{x}) for the competing hypotheses.

10.1.2 Bayes Factor

The Bayes factor summarises how strongly the data update our beliefs:

B_{10} = \frac{m_1(\underline{x})}{m_0(\underline{x})}.

It measures how much more (or less) the data support H_1 compared to H_0.

Posterior odds are obtained from prior odds via:

\frac{\mathrm{Pr}(H_1 \mid \underline{x})}{\mathrm{Pr}(H_0 \mid \underline{x})} = B_{10} \times \frac{\mathrm{Pr}(H_1)}{\mathrm{Pr}(H_0)}.

  • If B_{10} > 1: the data increase belief in H_1.
  • If B_{10} < 1: the data increase belief in H_0.

A Bayes factor is not itself a posterior probability: posterior odds also depend on the prior odds. Moreover, the marginal likelihood under a composite hypothesis depends on its conditional prior, which must be proper for the standard Bayes factor to be defined.

Explore a Bayes factor

Suppose our model is

X_i \mid \mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 10^2),

and we want to test whether \mu = 200:

  • H_0: \mu = 200
  • H_1: \mu \neq 200, with conditional prior \mu\mid H_1\sim\mathcal{N}(200,25).

We compare the likelihood of the observed sample mean under each hypothesis.

Predict. What should happen to B_{10} as \bar x moves away from 200? Then change both the observed mean and the prior scale under H_1.

n = 30
sigma = 10
mu0 = 200
sampling_sd = sigma / Math.sqrt(n)

viewof xbar_obs = Inputs.range([mu0 - 8*sampling_sd, mu0 + 8*sampling_sd],
  {step: 0.1, value: mu0, label: md`Observed sample mean ${tex`\bar{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}`}`} )

viewof prior_sd = Inputs.range([1, 20],
  {step: 0.5, value: 5, label: md`Prior standard deviation under ${tex`H_1`}`} )

// Likelihood under H0 (point null)
m0 = 1/(sigma/Math.sqrt(n)*Math.sqrt(2*Math.PI)) *
     Math.exp(-0.5*((xbar_obs - mu0)/(sigma/Math.sqrt(n)))**2)

// Marginal likelihood under H1 (proper normal conditional prior on mu)
tau = prior_sd**2

// Under H1, the marginal distribution of xbar is Normal(mu0, sigma^2/n + tau)
m1 = 1/Math.sqrt(2*Math.PI*(sigma**2/n + tau)) *
     Math.exp(-0.5*((xbar_obs - mu0)**2)/(sigma**2/n + tau))

// Bayes factor
B10 = m1/m0
posteriorH1EqualOdds = B10 / (1 + B10)

Observe and explain. Evidence depends both on the distance from the null and on how sharply H_1 predicted that distance. A more diffuse alternative does not automatically receive a larger Bayes factor.

Example 10.1 (Normal-Mean Bayes Factor: Complete Calculation) Use the model above with n=30, known \sigma=10 and observed \bar x=204. Under H_0:\mu=200, the sampling distribution of the mean is \bar X\mid H_0\sim\mathcal N\!\left(200,\frac{100}{30}\right). Under H_1, use the proper conditional prior \mu\mid H_1\sim\mathcal N(200,25). Find B_{10} and the posterior probability of H_1 when the hypotheses have equal prior probability.

Solution

Under H_0, the marginal density of \bar X at 204 is m_0(204) =\phi\!\left(204;200,\frac{100}{30}\right) \approx0.01982, where \phi(x;m,v) denotes the \mathcal N(m,v) density.

Under H_1, integrating over the Normal prior adds the prior variance: \bar X\mid H_1 \sim\mathcal N\!\left(200,\frac{100}{30}+25\right), so m_1(204) =\phi\!\left(204;200,\frac{100}{30}+25\right) \approx0.05651. Therefore B_{10}=\frac{m_1(204)}{m_0(204)} \approx\frac{0.05651}{0.01982} =2.85.

With equal prior odds, the posterior odds are 2.85:1 and \Pr(H_1\mid\underline{x}) =\frac{2.85}{1+2.85} \approx0.740.

For comparison, the frequentist statistic for the same data is Z=\frac{204-200}{10/\sqrt{30}}\approx2.19, with a two-sided p-value of approximately 0.0285. The p-value asks how unusual the statistic is under H_0; the Bayes factor compares the predictions made by H_0 and the specified H_1. Neither is the other in disguise.

Prior sensitivity and decisions. For the same \bar x=204, changing the prior variance under H_1 from 25 to 100 changes B_{10} from about 2.85 to 1.83. Report the alternative prior and justify its scale. A final accept/reject action additionally requires a decision rule or losses; evidence alone does not determine the consequences of being wrong.

TipChapter checkpoint

For a point-null problem, can you state the prior probability of each hypothesis, specify a proper conditional prior under the alternative, compute posterior odds from prior odds and a Bayes factor, and explain why the result is not a p-value?

10.2 Chapter summary

  • Bayesian point-null testing begins with prior probabilities for the hypotheses and proper conditional priors within them.
  • The Bayes factor compares how well the hypotheses predicted the observed data and updates prior odds to posterior odds.
  • A Bayes factor can favour either hypothesis, but its value depends on the model and prior specified under each.
  • A frequentist p-value is calibrated only under H_0 and should not be interpreted as a Bayes factor or posterior hypothesis probability.
