6.1 Introduction to the Bayesian Approach

So far we have taken a frequentist perspective: parameters such as \theta are fixed but unknown, and all randomness comes from the sample.

Bayesian inference takes a different view:

  • Frequentist: the parameter \theta is fixed but unknown.
  • Bayesian: the parameter \theta is treated as a random variable.

The word random here does not mean that the parameter is physically fluctuating. Instead, it represents our uncertainty about its true value.

6.1.1 Probability as Uncertainty

In the Bayesian framework, probability is interpreted as a way of quantifying uncertainty about unknown quantities.

Rather than saying “the parameter is a fixed number we don’t know”, we describe how plausible different values are using a probability distribution.

Example 6.1 (Umbrella Decisions) Imagine the weather forecast says there is a 70\% chance of rain tomorrow.

  • For a particular tomorrow, rain either occurs or it does not.

  • The number 70\% can express our uncertainty given current information; across well-calibrated forecasts issued at 70\%, rain should also occur about 70\% of the time.

6.1.2 The Bayesian Inference Procedure

The Bayesian approach to inference has three ingredients:

  1. Prior — a probability distribution that captures what we believe about \theta before seeing the data.
  2. Likelihood — the sampling model evaluated at the observed data, viewed as a function of \theta.
  3. Posterior — the updated distribution for \theta that combines prior information with the evidence from the data.

The update is performed using Bayes’ theorem (introduced in the next section). Intuitively:

\text{Posterior} \;\; \propto \;\; \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior}.

This equation expresses the central idea: our beliefs about \theta are revised in light of the observed data.

6.2 Bayes’ Theorem for Densities

Bayesian inference is named after Bayes’ Theorem 1.2, a general identity in probability theory. For two events A and B, the conditional probability is given by \mathrm{Pr}(A \mid B) = \frac{\mathrm{Pr}(B \mid A)\,\mathrm{Pr}(A)}{\mathrm{Pr}(B)}. This rule is sometimes described as reversing conditioning: it lets us swap the order, turning “B given A” into “A given B,” with a correction for their overall probabilities.

To apply this idea in Bayesian inference, we extend it from events to random variables and their densities. Using conditional densities 1.7, f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{f_{XY}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)},

we arrive at Bayes’ theorem for densities.

Theorem 6.1 (Bayes’ Theorem for Densities.) For random variables X and Y with joint density (or PMF) f(x,y):

f(x \mid y) = \frac{f(y \mid x)\,f(x)}{f(y)}.

NoteProof

Proof 6.1. By the definition of conditional density:

f(x \mid y) = \frac{f(x,y)}{f(y)}, \qquad f(y \mid x) = \frac{f(x,y)}{f(x)}.

Rearranging the second expression gives f(x,y) = f(y \mid x) f(x). Substituting this into the first gives

f(x \mid y) = \frac{f(y \mid x) f(x)}{f(y)}.

6.3 Bayes’ Theorem in Statistics

Once we have specified a parameter \theta, the model tells us the distribution of the data: f(\underline{x} \mid \theta).

This is the likelihood 5 (the joint density/PMF of the observed sample under \theta).

Our Bayesian goal is the reverse: to describe the distribution of the parameter given the data: \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}).

This is exactly the “reversing conditioning” step that Bayes’ theorem 1.2 makes possible. It takes the likelihood f(\underline{x}\mid \theta) and the prior \pi(\theta) and turns them into the posterior \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}): \text{Known: } f(\underline{x}\mid \theta) \quad\;\; \xRightarrow[\text{Bayes’ theorem}]{} \quad\;\; \text{Wanted: } \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x})

TipRemark (Likelihood vs. Likelihood Function)

Remark (Likelihood vs. Likelihood Function). Here we use likelihood to mean the joint density/PMF f(\underline{x}\mid\theta) itself.

When viewed as a function of \theta (with the data fixed), this is the likelihood function 5.1. In this course, we will not stress this distinction further.

Important 6.1: Notation in Bayesian Statistics

Both frequentist and Bayesian inference involve probability distributions for the data.

  • In the frequentist setting we write

    X_i \sim \mathrm{Distribution}(\theta),

    where \theta is a fixed but unknown parameter.

  • In the Bayesian setting we write

    X_i \mid \theta \sim \mathrm{Distribution}(\theta),

    where the conditioning bar \mid \theta emphasises that \theta itself is random, and we are describing the distribution of the data given a particular value of \theta.

In both cases, f(x_i \mid \theta) denotes the PDF/PMF of the data conditional on \theta.

The Bayesian addition: we also introduce probability distributions over the parameter \theta itself. To emphasise the difference in notation:

  • Prior distribution (before data): \pi(\theta)
  • Posterior distribution (after data): \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x})

In Bayesian inference there are therefore two distinct kinds of distributions, which our notation keeps separate:

  • Data distributions: f(x \mid \theta) describe how the data would look if we knew \theta.
  • Parameter distributions: \pi(\theta) and \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) describe our uncertainty about \theta.

6.3.1 From Bayes’ theorem to the Posterior

To apply Bayes’ theorem 6.1 in statistics we make the identification:

  • x = \theta : the parameter, treated as a random variable.
  • y = \underline{x} : the observed data.

This leads directly to the posterior distribution:

\pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) = \frac{f(\underline{x} \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{f(\underline{x})}.

Definition 6.1 (Prior Distribution.) The prior distribution represents our uncertainty about \theta before observing the data.

We denote its density (PDF or PMF) by

\pi(\theta).

Definition 6.2 (Posterior Distribution) The posterior distribution represents our uncertainty about \theta after observing the data.

It is defined by \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) = \frac{f(\underline{x} \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{f(\underline{x})}.

  • The likelihood f(\underline{x} \mid \theta) is the term where data and parameter appear together; it is this dependence that updates the prior into the posterior.
  • The prior \pi(\theta) reflects what we believed about \theta before seeing the data.
  • The denominator f(\underline{x})=\int_{\Theta}f(\underline{x}\mid\theta)\pi(\theta)\,d\theta is the prior predictive density (or evidence) for the observed data and normalises the posterior to integrate to 1.

6.3.2 Posteriors in Practice

We now work through a simple example to see how this update works.

Example 6.4 (Biased Coin) Let \theta = \Pr(\text{Head}) for a possibly biased coin.

  • Prior: Before seeing data, we choose a density that is flat on the \theta scale:

    \theta \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1) \equiv \mathrm{Beta}(1,1), \qquad \pi(\theta) = 1 \text{ for } 0<\theta<1.

    This prior has mean \mathrm{E}[\theta]=0.5.

    Calling this prior “uniform” describes its shape in this parameterisation; it is not automatically “no information,” because a transformation of \theta would not generally remain uniform.

  • Data: We toss the coin n=5 times and observe x=1 head.

  • Posterior: Bayes’ theorem combines the Binomial likelihood with the prior to give \pi(\theta \mid x).

Solution

Let X be the number of heads in 5 tosses. Assuming that each coin throw is independent and identically distributed, X is Binomial1: X \mid \theta \sim \textrm{Bin}(5,\theta). Note that there is only a single observation here: one experiment is “tossing the coin 5 times and seeing how many come up heads”. The probability of observing X=1 is f(x = 1 \mid \theta) = 5\theta(1-\theta)^4 If we plot this:

we see that this favours \theta around 0.2. In fact, in Example 5.9, we saw that the MLE is \hat{\theta} = 0.2.

Our prior for \theta was \pi(\theta) = 1, for 0 < \theta < 1. To update our beliefs, by conditioning on the single observation x=1, we use Bayes’ Theorem 6.2: \pi(\theta|x=1)=\frac{\pi(\theta)f(x=1|\theta)}{f(x=1)} = \frac{1 \times 5\theta(1-\theta)^4}{f(x=1)}, \quad 0 < \theta < 1. To compute the denominator, f(x=1), we use the law of total probability 1.3:

\begin{align*} f(x=1) &=\int_\Theta\pi(\theta)f(x=1\, |\,\theta)\,d\theta = \int_0^1 1\times 5\theta(1-\theta)^4\,d\theta \\ &=\int_0^1 \theta\times 5(1-\theta)^4\,d\theta =\left[-(1-\theta)^5\,\theta\right]^1_0 +\int_0^1 (1-\theta)^5\,d\theta \\ &=0 + \left[-\frac{(1-\theta)^6}{6}\right]^1_0 =\frac{1}{6}. \end{align*}

Therefore, the posterior density is (for 0<\theta<1):

\begin{align*} \pi(\theta|x=1)&=\frac{\pi(\theta)f(x=1|\theta)}{f(x=1)} =\frac{5\theta(1-\theta)^4}{1/6}\\ &=30\,\theta(1-\theta)^4 =\frac{\theta(1-\theta)^4}{\mathrm{B}(2,5)},\quad\quad 0<\theta<1, \end{align*}

and so the posterior distribution is \theta|x=1\sim \textrm{Beta}(2,5). This distribution has its mode at \theta=0.2, and mean at \mathrm{E}[\theta|x=1]=2/7=0.286.

Interpretation:

  • Most likely value (mode): \hat{\theta} \approx 0.2, the same as the sample proportion 1/5.
  • Posterior mean: \mathrm{E}[\theta \mid x] \approx 0.286, slightly larger than 0.2 because the prior was centred at 0.5.
  • Uncertainty reduced: the posterior standard deviation shrinks from 0.289 (prior) to 0.160 (posterior).

So, after seeing the data, our beliefs about \theta have been updated: we now think small values of \theta are more plausible, but not as extremely as the raw data alone would suggest.

Tip 6.1: Recurring Bernoulli example: frequentist and Bayesian estimates

For x successes in n Bernoulli trials, Chapter 4 studied \hat p=\bar Y=x/n through its bias and variance, and Chapter 5 showed that it is the MLE. With a \mathrm{Beta}(a,b) prior, p\mid x\sim\mathrm{Beta}(a+x,b+n-x). The three common point summaries are \hat p_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\frac{x}{n},\qquad \hat p_{\mathrm{mean}}=\frac{a+x}{a+b+n},\qquad \hat p_{\mathrm{MAP}}=\frac{a+x-1}{a+b+n-2}, where the MAP formula assumes an interior mode. The prior contributes pseudo-successes and pseudo-failures: a,b in the posterior mean, or a-1,b-1 in the MAP. As n grows with the prior fixed, the influence of these prior counts diminishes and all three estimates tend to agree.

The main difficulty in calculating the posterior distribution was in obtaining the f(x) term. However, in many cases we can recognise the posterior distribution without the need to calculate this constant term (constant with respect to \theta). In this example, we can calculate the posterior distribution as

\begin{align*} \pi(\theta|\underline{x})&\propto\pi(\theta)f(x=1|\theta) \\ &\propto 1\times 5\theta(1-\theta)^4,\quad\quad 0<\theta<1 \\ &=k\theta(1-\theta)^4,\quad\quad 0<\theta<1. \end{align*}

As \theta is a continuous quantity, what we would like to know is what continuous distribution defined on (0,1) has a probability density function which takes the form k\theta^{g-1}(1-\theta)^{h-1}. The answer is the \textrm{Beta}(g,h) distribution. Therefore, choosing g and h appropriately, we can see that the posterior distribution is \theta|x=1\sim \textrm{Beta}(2,5).

This leads us to following method of computing posteriors:

Important 6.2: Posterior Density Up to a Constant of Proportionality

In practice, when deriving Bayesian posteriors, we only need to identify the posterior density up to a constant of proportionality: \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) \;\propto\; f(\underline{x}\mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta).

The proportionality symbol \propto literally means that \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) = k\, f(\underline{x}\mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta), for some positive constant k>0 that does not depend on \theta.

This works because:

  1. The posterior density must integrate to 1: 1 = \int \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x})\, d\theta = k \int f(\underline{x}\mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)\, d\theta, which determines the normalisation constant: k = \left( \int f(\underline{x}\mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)\, d\theta \right)^{-1} = (f(\underline{x}))^{-1}.

  2. Since k does not depend on \theta, we may ignore it when identifying how the posterior depends on \theta.
    Therefore, we work with \pi(\theta\mid \underline{x}) \propto f(\underline{x}\mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta), and only include the normalising constant at the very end.

  3. When the expression on the right-hand side matches the functional form of a known distribution family (e.g., Normal, Gamma, Inverse Gamma), we can immediately identify the posterior distribution by comparing exponents, powers of \theta, and exponential terms.

  4. Once the form of the distribution is identified, the normalising constant is automatically supplied by the standard formula for that distribution.

TLDR: Working “up to proportionality” allows us to avoid directly computing the integral f(\underline{x}) = \int f(\underline{x}\mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)\, d\theta, which is often difficult or impossible to evaluate analytically. Instead, we focus only on the parts of the expression that involve \theta.

Example 6.5 Consider an experiment to determine how good a music expert is at distinguishing between pages from Haydn and Mozart scores. Let \theta=\mathrm{Pr}(\text{correct choice}). Suppose that, before conducting the experiment, we have been told that the expert is very competent. In fact, it is suggested that we should have a prior distribution which has a mode around \theta=0.95 and for which \mathrm{Pr}(\theta<0.8) is very small. We choose \theta\sim \textrm{Beta}(77,5), with probability density function \pi(\theta)=128107980\,\theta^{76}(1-\theta)^4,\quad\quad 0<\theta<1. A graph of this prior density is given as follows:

import numpy as np
import math
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

set_plot_theme("light")

# Beta(α, β) with α=77, β=5  →  π(θ) ∝ θ^(76) (1-θ)^(4)
alpha, beta = 77, 5

# B(α,β) = Γ(α)Γ(β)/Γ(α+β)  ⇒  1/B = exp(lgamma(α+β) - lgamma(α) - lgamma(β))
# Note the lgamma function computes the logarithm of the Gamma function.
beta_const_inv = math.exp(math.lgamma(alpha + beta) - math.lgamma(alpha) - math.lgamma(beta))

def beta_pdf(theta):
    return beta_const_inv * np.power(theta, alpha - 1) * np.power(1 - theta, beta - 1)

theta_grid = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 500)
pdf_values = beta_pdf(theta_grid)

plt.figure(figsize=(7,4))
plt.plot(theta_grid, pdf_values)
plt.xlabel(r"$\theta$")
plt.ylabel("Density")
plt.title(rf"Beta PDF: $\alpha={alpha}$, $\beta={beta}$")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

set_plot_theme("dark")

plt.figure(figsize=(7,4))
plt.plot(theta_grid, pdf_values)

  2. Compute the posterior distribution when the observed data consist of x = 5 correct responses out of n = 10 questions.
Solution
  1. We have \pi(\theta) = \frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}\theta^{a-1} (1-\theta)^{b-1} \propto \theta^{a-1} (1-\theta)^{b-1} and f(x \mid \theta) = \binom{n}{x} \theta^x(1-\theta)^{n-x} \propto \theta^x(1-\theta)^{n-x} Therefore \pi(\theta \mid x) \propto \theta^{a + x - 1} (1-\theta)^{b + n - x - 1} which takes the form of a \theta \mid x \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a + x, b+n-x) distribution.

The prior mean is 77/82\approx0.939, the data proportion is 5/10=0.5, and the posterior mean is 82/92\approx0.891. The posterior therefore remains close to the prior despite a clear prior–data conflict. With the much weaker \mathrm{Beta}(1,1) prior, the same data would give \mathrm{Beta}(6,6) with posterior mean 0.5. Reporting this comparison makes the influence of the prior visible rather than treating the update as purely mechanical.

Example 6.6 Software engineers at Instagram are interested in the click-through rate \theta of their advertisement recommender.

The click-through rate is the proportion of clicks out of the total number of impressions of an online advertisement.

The engineers assess the click-through rate by testing their recommender on users of Instagram. They obtain a total of n impressions.

  1. Using a Beta prior 3.2.11, quantify your prior beliefs about \theta.
  2. After running the test, the engineers obtained a total of X=47 successful clicks out of a total of n=10,000 impressions. Using a Binomial model and your prior specified in part (a), obtain the posterior distribution for \theta.
Solution

a.

Suppose previous campaigns suggest that click-through rates are low, but there is still substantial uncertainty. As an illustrative, moderately informative prior, take

\theta \sim \text{Beta}(5,\,100).

This prior has mean

\frac{5}{5+100} \approx 0.047,

and places the vast majority of its probability mass on values \theta < 0.1.

Its concentration 5+100=105 is comparable to roughly one hundred prior Bernoulli observations in the posterior-mean calculation, so it should not be described as a nearly information-free prior.

b.

The data model is

X \mid \theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n=10000,\, \theta), \qquad X = 47,

which has PMF

f(x=47 \mid \theta) = \binom{10000}{47} \theta^{47}(1-\theta)^{9953}.

Using Bayes’ theorem:

\pi(\theta \mid x) \;\propto\; f(x \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta).

From part (a) the prior is

\pi(\theta) \;\propto\; \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}, \qquad \alpha=5, \;\beta=100.

Therefore,

\pi(\theta \mid x) \;\propto\; \theta^{47}(1-\theta)^{9953}\;\cdot\;\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}.

Simplifying exponents:

\pi(\theta \mid x) \;\propto\; \theta^{(\alpha+47)-1}(1-\theta)^{(\beta+9953)-1}.

Thus, the posterior distribution is

\theta \mid x \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha+47, \; \beta + 9953).

Substituting \alpha=5, \beta=100:

\theta \mid X \sim \text{Beta}(5+47, \; 100 + 9953) = \text{Beta}(52, \; 10053).

Interpretation.

  • The posterior mean is

\mathrm{E}[\theta \mid X] = \frac{52}{52+10053} \approx 0.0052,

which is about 0.52% click-through rate.

  • With a weak \mathrm{Beta}(1,1) prior, the posterior mean would be 48/10002\approx0.0048, close to both the observed rate 47/10000=0.0047 and the result above. This sensitivity comparison shows that the large dataset dominates these two priors.

  • Conditional on the i.i.d. Binomial model and a representative sample, the posterior variance is very small because n=10{,}000 is large. A large n reduces sampling uncertainty but does not repair dependence between impressions, selection bias, or changes in click-through rate across users and adverts.

Example 6.7 (Measuring a calibration signal) You are attempting to measure an unknown calibration signal level \theta and quantify the uncertainty in the measurement. Repeated instrument readings are denoted by X_i\mid\theta, for i=1,\ldots,n, in arbitrary signal units.

Many small factors contribute additively to the measurement error.

(a) Using a normal prior, quantify your prior beliefs about \theta.

Solution (a)

Suppose historical calibrations suggest a signal level around 150{,}000, with substantial between-run uncertainty. A possible prior is

\theta \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(1.5\times 10^5,\ (4\times 10^4)^2\big).

This prior makes its scale and uncertainty explicit. A different scientific context would require a different elicitation; in particular, a parameter with hard physical bounds should not be assigned an unconstrained Normal prior without checking the mass outside those bounds.

(b) Why is a normal distribution appropriate for the likelihood? Write down the likelihood.

Solution (b)

Because many small, additive, independent sources of error contribute to each measurement, the Central Limit Theorem 4.1 motivates

X_i \mid \theta \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^2).

Hence the likelihood is

\begin{align*} f(\underline{x}\mid \theta,\sigma) &= (2\pi)^{-n/2}\sigma^{-n} \exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\theta)^2\right\} \\ &= (2\pi)^{-n/2}\sigma^{-n} \exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2 - 2\theta \sum_{i=1}^n x_i + n\theta^2\Big)\right\}. \end{align*}

(c) Assuming the standard deviation \sigma of the measurements is known, derive the posterior under a prior \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2).

Solution (c)

Up to proportionality,

f(\underline{x}\mid\theta,\sigma)\ \propto\ \exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\big(n\theta^2-2n\bar{x}\,\theta\big)\right\},\qquad \pi(\theta)\ \propto\ \exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_0^2}\big(\theta^2-2\mu_0\theta\big)\right\}.

Multiplying and completing the square, the posterior is normal with precision (inverse variance)

\frac{1}{V}=\frac{n}{\sigma^2}+\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}, \qquad M = V\left(\frac{n\bar{x}}{\sigma^2}+\frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2}\right).

Equivalently, M=w\bar x+(1-w)\mu_0, \qquad w=\frac{n/\sigma^2}{n/\sigma^2+1/\sigma_0^2}. The posterior mean is therefore a precision-weighted average of the data mean and prior mean: the more precise source receives more weight. This is Bayesian shrinkage in a directly visible form.

Therefore,

\boxed{\ \theta\mid \underline{x}\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}(M,V)\ }.

(d) Suppose you measured the signal 4 times and obtained: 306135,\ 293227,\ 307985,\ 301298. Using the prior from (a), set \sigma=5000 and derive your posterior distribution.

Solution (d)

Compute \bar{x}=(306135+293227+307985+301298)/4=302{,}161.25. With \mu_0=150{,}000, \sigma_0^2=(40{,}000)^2, \sigma^2=(5{,}000)^2, n=4:

\frac{1}{V}=\frac{n}{\sigma^2}+\frac{1}{\sigma_0^2} =\frac{4}{25{,}000{,}000}+\frac{1}{1{,}600{,}000{,}000} =1.60625\times 10^{-7}, \quad V=\frac{1}{1.60625\times 10^{-7}}\approx 6{,}225{,}681.

M = V\left(\frac{n\bar{x}}{\sigma^2}+\frac{\mu_0}{\sigma_0^2}\right) \approx 6{,}225{,}681\left(\frac{4\cdot 302{,}161.25}{25{,}000{,}000}+\frac{150{,}000}{1{,}600{,}000{,}000}\right) \approx 301{,}569.18.

Thus,

\boxed{\ \theta\mid \underline{x}\ \sim\ \mathcal{N}\!\big(301{,}569.2,\ 6{,}225{,}681\big)\ } \quad \text{(i.e., SD } \approx 2{,}495.1\text{ signal units)}.

The posterior is far from the prior mean. That may be genuine learning, but it should also prompt a model check: did the calibration regime change, was the prior based on a comparable instrument, and are the stated measurement errors credible? The calculation is conditional on those modelling choices.

6.3.3 Conjugacy

In all the examples in Posteriors in Practice 6.3.2, a notable phenomenon occurred:

  1. The prior distribution \pi(\theta)
  2. The resulting posterior distribution \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x})

belonged to the same family of distributions.

In Bayesian inference, this property is called conjugacy, and it allows us to derive posterior distributions in closed form without approximation.

Definition 6.3 (Conjugate Prior) Suppose f(\underline{x} \mid \theta) is the joint PDF/PMF of the observations.

A prior distribution for \theta is said to be conjugate if, after updating with the data, the posterior distribution \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) remains in the same family as the prior \pi(\theta).

TipRemark (Why is conjugacy useful?)

Remark 6.1 (Why is conjugacy useful?).

  • Conjugacy makes Bayesian updating algebraically simple: posterior parameters can often be obtained by just “updating counts” or “adding observations”.
  • This provides intuition for how prior information and data combine, and gives closed-form formulas that are easy to compute.

Since every example in this course uses a conjugate prior, posterior computation follows a simple and repeatable recipe.
Conjugacy guarantees that the posterior has the same family as the prior, so once you recognise the form, the calculation becomes mechanical.

Important 6.3: Computing Posterior Distributions

  1. Compute the Likelihood.
    For data generated from a model X_i \mid \theta \sim \mathrm{Distribution}(\theta), the likelihood in the i.i.d. case is f(\underline{x} \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \theta), where f(x_i \mid \theta) is the PDF (or PMF) of each observation.

  2. Write Down the Prior.
    Given a prior \theta \sim \mathrm{Distribution}(\phi), write down its density \pi(\theta).

  3. Form the Unnormalised Posterior.
    Multiply the prior and likelihood: \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) \propto \pi(\theta)\, f(\underline{x} \mid \theta), and discard any terms that do not depend on \theta.

  4. Identify the Posterior Family.
    Because all priors used in this course are conjugate 6.3, the resulting expression will match the same distributional form as the prior.

    Read off the updated parameters to obtain the posterior.

6.4 Bayesian Estimators

In Bayesian inference, point estimators are obtained by summarising the posterior distribution \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) = \frac{f(\underline{x}\mid\theta)\, \pi(\theta)}{f(\underline{x})}.

As in Chapter 4, an estimator is a rule depending on the random data \underline X; after observing \underline x, its numerical output is an estimate. We often choose a single value to summarise the posterior, but the appropriate summary depends on how estimation errors will be judged. Different loss functions lead to different Bayesian estimators. Three common examples are:

  1. Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) Estimator:
    Estimate \theta using the mode of the posterior: \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\theta}\, \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}).

  2. Posterior Mean:
    Estimate \theta using the average of the posterior: \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{mean}} = \mathrm{E}[\theta \mid \underline{x}].

  3. Posterior Median:
    Estimate \theta using the middle of the posterior distribution - the point at which the posterior assigns equal probability to being below or above: \mathrm{Pr}(\theta \le \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{median}} \mid \underline{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2}.

Among these, the MAP estimator is particularly important and is given its own formal definition below.

TipPoint summaries and loss
  • Under squared-error loss, the posterior mean minimises posterior expected loss. This connects directly to the MSE criterion in Chapter 4.
  • Under absolute-error loss, the posterior median is optimal.
  • The MAP selects the highest posterior density and can be motivated by an increasingly narrow zero-one-style loss.

There is therefore no universally best posterior summary without stating the decision goal. In particular, for a continuous parameter the MAP is a density mode, not a point with positive posterior probability, and it can change under reparameterisation.

Definition 6.4 (Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) Estimator) The MAP estimator is the value of \theta that maximises the posterior density: \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\theta}\, \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}).

Since the denominator f(\underline{x}) does not depend on \theta, we may equivalently write: \hat{\theta}_{\mathrm{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\theta}\, f(\underline{x}\mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta).

Thus, the MAP estimator is the value of \theta that is most plausible after observing the data.

Important 6.4: Computing the MAP estimator

To compute a MAP estimator, we follow almost exactly the same steps used to compute an MLE.
The only difference is that the likelihood is replaced by the posterior.

  1. Write the posterior (up to proportionality):
    Since the normalising constant does not depend on \theta, we work with \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) \propto f(\underline{x}\mid\theta)\,\pi(\theta).

  2. Take logarithms:
    The logarithm turns products into sums, simplifying differentiation: \log \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) = C + \log f(\underline{x}\mid\theta) + \log \pi(\theta), where C is a constant not depending on \theta.

  3. Find interior candidates: Differentiate with respect to \theta and set the derivative equal to zero: \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\,\log \pi(\theta \mid \underline{x}) = 0. Solutions are stationary-point candidates, not automatically maxima.

  4. Check the global maximum: Compare candidates, verify curvature or the global shape, and inspect the boundaries of the parameter space. The MAP is the value that actually maximises the posterior.

In short:

  • compute the posterior up to proportionality,
  • take logs,
  • differentiate,
  • find candidates and check which is the global maximiser, including boundaries.

This parallels the MLE procedure, except that the prior contributes an extra \log\pi(\theta) term. With a prior that is constant on the chosen parameter scale, an interior MAP and MLE coincide; with an informative prior they generally differ.

6.5 Asymptotic Posterior Distribution

In frequentist statistics, we saw two important results about the asymptotic 4.2 sampling distribution of estimators:

  1. The Central Limit Theorem 4.1 showed that the sampling distribution of the sample mean 4.1 is approximately Normal for large n.
  2. The Asymptotic Distribution of the MLE 5.3 showed that the sampling distribution of the maximum likelihood estimator is approximately Normal for large n.

A similar phenomenon occurs in Bayesian inference: in a regular, identifiable, fixed-dimensional model, the posterior distribution becomes approximately Normal when the prior has positive, smooth density near the true parameter. This can fail at boundaries, in non-identifiable models, or when the prior rules out the truth.

This important result is known as the Bernstein–von Mises theorem. However, we will refer to it simply as the asymptotic posterior distribution:

Theorem 6.2 (Asymptotic Posterior Distribution) Suppose we have an i.i.d. sample X_1,\ldots,X_n drawn from the same distribution with PDF/PMF f(x \mid \theta) and let \pi(\theta \mid \underline{X}^{(n)}) denote the posterior distribution for \theta.

Under regularity conditions, as n \to \infty the posterior distribution satisfies \theta \mid \underline{X}^{(n)} \;\overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\Big(\hat{\theta}_n,\, \mathcal{I}_n(\theta_0)^{-1}\Big), where

  • \hat{\theta}_n is the MLE,
  • \theta_0 is the true parameter value,
  • \mathcal{I}_n(\theta_0) is the Fisher information 5.1 for the whole sample, evaluated at \theta_0.

Since \theta_0 is unknown, in practice we use the plug-in approximation \theta \mid \underline{X}^{(n)} \;\overset{\text{approx.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\Big(\hat{\theta}_n,\, \mathcal{I}_n(\hat{\theta}_n)^{-1}\Big), obtained by replacing \theta_0 with \hat{\theta}_n in the Fisher information.

ImportantSame Normal shape, different probability statements

The MLE approximation in Chapter 5 describes the sampling distribution of \hat\theta_n when new datasets are repeatedly generated with \theta_0 fixed. The approximation above describes the posterior distribution of \theta conditional on the dataset that was observed. Under regularity conditions they have approximately the same centre and information-based variance, but they condition on different things and support different interpretations.

6.6 Chapter summary

  • Bayesian updating combines a likelihood shared with frequentist inference and an explicit prior to produce a posterior distribution.
  • Conjugacy makes some updates algebraic, but prior strength, parameter support, and model assumptions still require interpretation.
  • The posterior mean, median, and mode answer different decision questions; there is no universally best point summary without a loss or decision goal.
  • Large samples often reduce prior influence and can produce an approximately Normal posterior, but the result is conditional on the observed data and can fail outside regular settings.
